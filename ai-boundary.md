## 2. The boundary problem

The debate over AI consciousness is normally conducted as if its subject were well defined. Participants ask
of a given system whether it is conscious and disagree about the answer. We argue that some of the disagreement 
is the result of a lack of a shared idea of a referent of "the system". 
The candidates on offer are distinct objects with different
boundaries, different persistence conditions, and capable of sustaining different processes. 
Until a candidate is fixed, the attribution question is not well posed.
As a first step to assessing a candidate system for consciousness,
it is necessary to identify two of its features, both of which are not trivially defensible requirements.

### 2.1 Fixing a referent

**Feature 1: the physical boundary.**
Assuming that consciousness requires a referent, i.e. some
bounded matter to which the property is attributed, is a choice we make here 
before posing the attribution question. This
is not the only available view. Panpsychists take (proto-)experience to be ubiquitous at the fundamental
physical level. The consciousness question then becomes "how do already-present micro-experience combines 
into a unified macro-subject?" (Chalmers 2016).
We set aside panpsychism, along with any other theories that do not require a bounded system as a referent, 
as adjudicating the presence of a referent is outside the scope of this work. 

**Feature 2: the process.**
Whether base criteria for locating consciousness can be expressed in terms of a physical process, state, or 
property is a live dispute in the metaphysics of mind 
(machine functionalism (Putnam 1967) speaks of
mental states; Chalmers (1996) develops a property view). 
Integrated information (Albantakis et al. 2023) and
measures of neural subcriticality (Algom and Shriki 2026), both proposed as criteria for locating consciousness,
are themselves states, not processes, on their own terms. Such criteria, however, are conditions for 
locating consciousness, rather than the experiential phenomenon itself.
What we call a "process" here is the physical persistence of the conscious experience.
Felt experience is necessarily had for a duration of time, 
even when the theory picking out its physical basis defines that basis in static terms. 
In a physicalist framework, the physical realization of felt experience is not the state or property itself,
but what occurs, as time passes, in virtue of that state or property being true at a preceding point 
in time. When proposing a candidate system, this is the process we must demonstrate.

**Circularity.**
Fixing a referent looks circular on its face: arbitrating among candidate boundaries seems to require a
definition of consciousness already in hand, so the question of which system to evaluate and the question of
whether that system is conscious appear impossible to settle independently of one another. This circularity is
not new, and existing work already shows a way through it.

### 2.2 Two precedents for the referent constraints

The search for neural correlates of consciousness makes constraints on the referent 
explicit in two distinct traditions. 
On Chalmers's
(2000) definition, a neural correlate of consciousness is a minimal neural system N whose state is sufficient,
under specified background conditions, for a corresponding state of consciousness, a formulation that
presupposes N's boundary has already been drawn before any such sufficiency claim could even be tested. Crick
and Koch (1990, 2003) pursue the same search for a minimal
sufficient physical substrate. This approach searches for the answer to 
"what physical state correlates with experience?".
A second, independent tradition is called theory of biological autonomy.
It asks "what makes
a physical system a self-sustaining unit capable of bearing the property of having consciousness?".  
For Maturana and Varela (1980), a system counts as *autopoietic*, and thus a candidate locus for
cognition, only when a network of processes produces the components that make it up, and those components both
regenerate the network and form its physical boundary.
There, process and boundary are not independently specifiable, and each is
defined through the other. This is what breaks the circularity: the boundary is fixed by whether a process
closes into a self-maintaining organization, a fact checkable independently of any prior theory of what
consciousness is, and prior to any judgment about whether the result turns out to be conscious. 

Both traditions treat a process without a specified physical boundary as not yet a
candidate for consciousness, disagreeing only on the criteria for the process and the boundary. 
Without these specific constraints of either tradition, their claims generalize to a relatively 
uncontroversial statement. This statement is:
"a specific, bounded piece of physical matter capable of supporting the 
consciousness process must be identified as its site" (Putnam 1967). This is a distinct statement from
"only some specific type of physical matter can support consciousness". The latter 
is usually referred to as *substrate dependence*, and is heavily contested. Some argue that only
the brain's specific biological causal powers can produce consciousness (Searle 1992), while others
argue that consciousness depends only on functional organization, independently of its physical
realizer (Chalmers 1995).

### 2.3 Process integration

Both traditions in Section 2.2 agree that a bounded piece of matter must be identified as the site of
consciousness. That agreement does not say how to identify it, and the biological-autonomy tradition proposes 
self-maintenance as the identification criterion. 
Self-maintenance is necessary, but not sufficient, since a self-maintaining system can enclose
more than the disputed process, or it can additionally be maintained by machinery external to that process. In either
case, self-maintenance alone does not pick out the boundary of the disputed process. A sharper tool is the
notion of a constraint (Montévil and Mossio 2015).
Given a process A→B, C is a constraint on that process at timescale T iff (i) C exerts a causal action that
shapes A→B, and (ii) C remains invariant through that action at timescale T , for example:

  - an enzyme binds the reacting molecules and lowers the energy barrier between them, so the reaction runs
    faster; the enzyme itself comes out of the reaction unchanged;
  - a membrane lets only some molecules through, so it sets which molecules diffuse where; the membrane persists
    while they do.

Some constraints, including the ones above, reproduce the object which enforces the constraint. 
The enzyme is resynthesized by the metabolism it catalyzes. The
membrane is rebuilt from materials that diffusion supplies. Montévil and Mossio (2015) call this *closure of
constraints*. In an open, non-equilibrium system, a set of constraints achieves closure when each
constraint is produced by another in the set and helps produce another in turn. Closure is a specific form of
self-maintenance, but it is still necessary yet insufficient for identifying a reasonable boundary. 
A flame maintains itself, since its heat vaporizes the wax that keeps it burning, so that it is a
single structure acting on its own conditions, but it lacks closure (Moreno and Mossio 2015). 
The boundary closure produced excludes both the part that no constraint in the set produces, and that produces 
none of them. This handles both failures of self-maintenance noted above.
A closed system
may lack any means of responding as it nears the limits of its viability. Di Paolo (2005) adds this capacity
to autopoiesis (Section 2.2) 
and calls it *adaptivity*: the system registers threats to its own viability and regulates its
behavior accordingly. Moreno and Mossio (2015) add the same capacity to closure of constraints, as regulation.
A system with closure and adaptivity (regulation) is *autonomous* in this tradition's sense. 
Autonomy, however, does not settle whether the disputed process takes part in the system's self-maintenance.
*Process integration*, the term introduced here, requires that it does. The system's self-maintenance is a
network of processes, through which the closed set maintains, adapts, regulates, and regenerates itself.
Process integration requires the disputed process to be one of them: constraints in the set act on it, and it
produces or maintains constraints in the set.

Two cases show the definition at work. In the nervous system, synaptic transmission triggers the plasticity
that rebuilds the synapse carrying it (Citri and Malenka 2008). Metabolism and signaling are coupled at every
scale. No module maintains the brain while a different one computes in it (Harris, Jolivet and Attwell 2012).
The nervous system satisfies process integration. Computation in an ordinary computer fails it. The chips and
their connections constrain the computation. They are fixed at manufacture, and the computation neither
produces nor maintains them. The constraints exist, and closure fails. Self-maintaining robots and neural
implants complicate this picture, and Section 4 treats them separately.

The nervous system also shows a practical consequence of process integration: dense internal coupling. A
network that continuously regenerates its own components does so through many concurrent pathways. Systems
whose maintaining and computing processes produce one another therefore tend to be coupled densely and at
every scale (Moreno and Mossio 2015). A general principle secures this. A constraint that produces and
maintains structure at some resolution must track variation in that structure at the same resolution. This
is an instance of Ashby's law of requisite variety: only a regulator with enough internal variety can hold a
coupled variable steady against disturbance (Ashby 1956). A coarse, serial channel therefore cannot in general
supply what reciprocal production and maintenance requires, whatever substrate it runs on. Process
integration thus implies high and consistent internal bandwidth. Because the argument does not depend on
substrate, the bandwidth diagnostic extends beyond biology to the mixed and social cases of Section 4.

A second consequence concerns copying a system's state. Suppose a complete description of a conscious
system's microstate, down to every channel and synapse, could be read out and used elsewhere. Three cases
follow. In the first, the description drives the construction of a new physical system whose computation is
unified with its maintenance from the outset. The result is a new individual that satisfies process
integration, whatever its relation to the original. In the second, the description is loaded as a simulation
onto general-purpose, reprogrammable hardware. The result fails, for the reason given in the computer case:
the hardware's power supply, cooling, and replaceable parts remain untouched by what it computes. In the
third, a simulation trained on such hardware becomes so entangled with the hardware's physical quirks that its
state can no longer be transferred elsewhere without loss. Nothing about the hardware has been fixed into
single-purpose form. Engineering has reached this third case from the other direction. Hinton (2022) observes
that learning which exploits device-specific quirks yields knowledge that dies with the device, and calls
such computation mortal. Ororbia and Friston (2023) build a theory of mortal computation on the free-energy
principle. Kleiner (2024) argues that computational functionalism, consistently applied, places
consciousness in the mortal class. Mortality is necessary for process integration. The third case shows that
it is insufficient. The trained hardware's maintenance remains addressable and replaceable without any change
to its structure. Practical non-portability leaves that fact untouched. Every system currently called an AI,
mortal or not, sits on the reprogrammable side of this line.

With the criterion and its consequences in place, it can be located among neighbouring views. Section 2.2
introduced the dispute over substrate dependence. Its two extremes differ on what a system must supply, beyond
computing the right function, in order to be conscious. Computationalism holds that the right input-output
profile suffices, whatever the physical realization. Biological naturalism holds that some feature of living
matter is necessary (Searle 1992; Seth 2025). Process integration sits between the extremes. It requires more
than function, and it requires no particular kind of matter. Several other accounts also sit between them.
Piccinini (2015) requires a genuine mechanism that manipulates medium-independent vehicles. Chalmers (1994;
1996) requires a combinatorial-state automaton whose counterfactual structure mirrors the computation's formal
structure. Wiese (2024) argues from the free-energy principle that computation suffices for consciousness only
within self-organizing dynamics bounded by a Markov blanket. Thompson's (2007) deep continuity thesis ties
mind to literal metabolic self-production. An ordinary computer clears Piccinini's and Chalmers's bars. It
fails Wiese's, because a von Neumann architecture separates memory from processing and cannot sustain the
causal flow he requires. It fails Thompson's, because its power supply, cooling, and replaceable parts remain
untouched by what it computes. Process integration is closest to Wiese's account. Both place dynamical demands
on a system, where Piccinini and Chalmers place mechanistic ones. Wiese applies the demand to the system's
computational organization. Process integration applies it to the maintaining and computing processes
together. Vieira and Gabora (2026) also treat constraint closure as necessary for machine consciousness. They
locate closure in autocatalytic networks of representations. On their diagnosis, current systems achieve at
most transient, task-bound closure. Process integration requires the loop to close between material
self-maintenance and computation, whatever architecture realizes it.

Process integration thus fixes a referent: a process together with the boundary of the constraint network
that sustains it. Current AI systems fall outside any such referent. Whether some wider boundary drawn around
them could change that verdict is an instance of the system boundary problem.

### 2.4 The system boundary problem

Once a referent is fixed, disagreement about consciousness may still remain. Here, we make the point that  
two questions are conflated: (1) the identification of the referent (process together with the 
substrate boundary, i.e. the system), 
and (2) the identification of whether the specified system sustains consciousness. 
This system boundary problem has been studied extensively. Fekete, van Leeuwen and Edelman
(2016) posed it for computational theories generally; it also figures in the
extended-mind literature (Clark and Chalmers 1998; Chalmers 2019; Kirchhoff and Kiverstein
2019; Facchin and Negro 2023), which often treats the relevant coupling as a matter of degree rather than kind
(we analyze the way this applies to AI in Section 6), and in the "mark of the cognitive" debate, which instead
seeks a principled line between processes that constitute cognition and those that merely causally support it
(Adams and Aizawa 2001). 
These discussions seek to fix the correct boundary by means of a measure, a criterion of direct 
availability, or a dynamical argument. The question (2) can only be posed meaningfully 
if and when a defensible boundary can been specified.

### 2.5 Existing boundary disputes

Boundary disputes of this kind have been studied in other fields of inquiry. 
In the philosophy of biology, we can point to the following: 
Darwinian individuality and its gradations (Godfrey-Smith 2009), policing and
demarcation criteria (Clarke 2010), cooperation and conflict as axes of organismality (Queller and Strassmann
2009), and information-theoretic measures on which individuality is quantitative and comes in degrees
(Krakauer et al. 2020). In most cases, boundaries are determined by
organization, and the tools for detecting them are usually relative to whatever model one adopts of the thing
whose boundary is in question. 
The Markov blanket is the standard example: a blanket is a
property of a statistical model of a system rather than of the system itself, and different modeling choices
place blankets in different locations. Kirchhoff and Kiverstein (2021) propose using it to fix the boundary of
the mind; Bruineberg et al. (2022) distinguish the blanket as an *instrument of
inference* from the blanket as a *metaphysical boundary of the mind*, and show how easily the free-energy
literature, including this proposal, slides from the first use to the second. Thus, a defensible choice of model according to which 
the proposed boundary is drawn is required.

## 3. Are viruses alive?

### 3.1 The problem of defining life

Biology faced a version of the problem of defining life.
The problem was dissolved by posing the organization-first
question adopted above prior to agreeing on the definition of life. 
What biology adds beyond this general approach is a specific example of a resolution following 
the adoption of this boundary-first approach. The decades-long dispute
over whether viruses are alive has not been answered directly. However, applying the method 
of first *locating a non-circularly-defined boundary* allowed a related, more meaningful 
question to be addressed. That this precedent should transfer to mind at all is itself independently
defended by the life-mind continuity thesis, on which mind is not a separate kind of phenomenon but a further
elaboration of the same organizational properties that constitute life (Thompson 2007; Di Paolo 2005).

### 3.2 The virocell

**A century-long stalemate.**
For most of a century biology hosted a dispute with the same shape as the present one.
The question of whether viruses are alive survived the accumulation of an incredible amount of 
seemingly relevant information obtained experimentally. Stanley's crystallization of tobacco mosaic virus in
1935 demonstrated that a virion can be purified and stored like a chemical reagent, yet upon
encountering a cell it initiates replication, heredity, and adaptation. 
In 1976, the full genome of a virus, the RNA bacteriophage MS2, was sequenced for the first 
time (Fiers et al. 1976). Still, the new information did not resolve the debate.
On metabolic definitions viruses fell outside life, and on replicator definitions
they fell inside (Villarreal 2004; Koonin and Starokadomskyy 2016), but 
no observation could arbitrate between criteria.

**The virocell.**
Progress was made by introducing the virocell and relocating the 
question to this object. The virocell is a cell whose own ribosomes, own
membranes, and own energy metabolism execute the viral program (Forterre 2010, 2011, 2013).
The virion itself is a dispersal stage, functionally comparable to a spore or a
seed, whereas the virocell is the living form of the virus.
The virocell is decisive because it satisfies, simultaneously, every criterion that had been proposed as
criterial for life. Three such criteria were already in play in the
biological literature:

1. **Metabolic self-maintenance.** A metabolizing, energy-transducing system that keeps itself away from
   equilibrium.
2. **Replication and heredity.** A system that produces copies of a heritable genetic program.
3. **Evolution by variation and selection.** A lineage whose heritable program changes under selection across
   replication cycles.

The virocell satisfies each of these, whereas the virion does not. A purified virion is chemically inert and
storable like a reagent, whereas the virocell's own ribosomes, membranes, and energy metabolism are running,
exactly as they would be in an uninfected cell, which satisfies (1). The virion is inert cargo until it enters a cell, while the
virocell copies and packages viral genomes into progeny virions, satisfying (2). And a single virion is a dispersal-stage
particle, not itself a locus of variation and selection, whereas the lineage of virocells it initiates is, 
satisfying (3).

**Takeover.**
A fourth criterion, takeover, is implicit in Forterre's own distinction between the virocell and
the merely infected but still-dividing "ribovirocell" (Forterre 2013), and we make it explicit here as a
separable criterion:

4. **Takeover.** The host's own maintaining machinery must be redirected wholesale to the disputed process,
   rather than merely coexisting with it. Once infected, the cell's transcriptional and translational apparatus
   stops serving its own reproduction and is redirected to manufacturing virions, so nothing of the host's
   original program continues untouched.

Takeover is not restricted to cases of forceful or parasitic redirection, however. Under ordinary conditions, an
uninfected cell's own machinery is already devoted to replicating its own genome, the same genome that built
that machinery in the first place. When we need to distinguish this from the parasitic case, we call it
*copacetic takeover*: the process that established the machinery and the process now directing it are one and
the same.

In the case of DNA-based living organisms, then, both copacetic and parasitic takeover is said to be achieved within a
boundary whenever a consistent (or, at least, modulo slight mutations) genome is the sole genome being read and
replicated: the virocell is one instance of the parasitic case. Retroviruses still have DNA-based virocells:
their RNA genome is reverse-transcribed into a DNA copy that integrates into the host genome (Baltimore 1970;
Temin and Mizutani 1970).

The takeover condition is what licenses using this particular boundary, the infected cell, rather than a smaller or larger one:
the disputed process has captured exactly the machinery the boundary encloses. Criteria (2) and (3) do no
individuating work of their own, they merely specify what the disputed process, viral propagation, must entail for
the property of being alive to be at stake. That is
why (2) and (3) are specific for adjudication of whether takeover was achieved for the process of life specifically,
and do not transfer to a debate about some
other disputed process, whereas (1) and (4) generalize as a schema for locating the boundary for other processes 
under consideration (such as computation, in our case).

**Demonstrating process integration.**
The virocell satisfies process integration (Section 2.3): the very metabolic processes criterion (1) picks
out are, upon infection, redirected wholesale to executing the heritable, evolving program specified by
criteria (2) and (3), rather than merely hosting it alongside its own. Transcription, translation, and
energy metabolism, previously in the business of maintaining the cell, are now in the business of copying
viral genomes and packaging virions, with nothing of the original activity continuing untouched. The virion
cannot itself supply such a network, whereas the virocell can.

**The residual dispute.**
The virocell discovery did not affect the overall debate about 
whether the criteria (1)-(3) for identifying life are both necessary and sufficient. Cleland and
Chyba (2002) argued that definitional disputes about life presuppose a theory of life we do not possess, so
that defining the term in advance of the theory is idle. Machery (2012) argued that "life" is a cluster concept
and the definitional project should simply be dropped. 
Farnsworth (2021) works from the same organizational tradition invoked in Section 2, on which a living system
must be closed to efficient causation: it must supply, from within itself, all the organization, material, and
energy its own reproduction requires. No virus meets this standard across its full replication cycle, Farnsworth
argues, because the virion always draws that organization, material, and energy from the host rather than
supplying them itself. 
His target, however, is the virus as an entity spanning both stages, virion and virocell together: producing
virions is not itself reproduction, since a virion cannot become a new virocell without capturing another
host cell's machinery, and that capture is precisely the wider, multi-generational cycle Farnsworth has in mind.
The narrower
claim defended here, that the infected cell is alive while being taken over, still stands, since it concerns
only the currently metabolizing virocell, not the further step of whether its progeny go on to find a host. 
Similarly, what is alive, the virocell, is a form of the virus in the sense 
that the process this physical organization now carries out is fully and 
exclusively specified by the virus's genome. 
The settlement generalizes smoothly across intermediate cases, and we use the resolutions arrived at via the 
generalization from the virocell example to adjudicate the AI consciousness candidates. 

### 3.3 The precedent in general form, applied to the virocell

The precedent can now be stated in general form, for the kind of property this paper is concerned with: 
one whose criteria are satisfied by some process occurring, rather than by a static state or structure.
Life and consciousness are our two instances of such a property. 
In each case above, a dispute attributes to some part, a
virion, a single organ, a property that part cannot exhibit alone, because the property is manifested only by
a larger composite containing it. This is a version of what Bennett and Hacker (2003) call the mereological
fallacy: ascribing to a part a predicate that is only intelligible applied to the whole. Diagnosing the fallacy
is not yet a method for finding the right whole, however; that is the gap this paper's conditions are meant to
fill. The move in each case is to relocate the question of whether a property 
is satisfied from the part to that composite. This
relocation settles the original dispute only if the following conditions are met. Conditions 2a and 2b below
are both defensible readings of the same requirement, but we adopt 2b throughout what follows: it keeps the
property's criteria anchored to the very locus where condition 1's process integration holds, rather than
letting them be satisfied piecemeal by parts of the composite that process integration says nothing about. 2a
is stated because it is the more permissive fallback a critic might press if 2b turns out too strong for some
case, but the arguments below rely only on 2b:

1.  **Process integration** (defended in Section 2): the process that carries out whatever function the
    disputed property requires belongs to the network of processes that maintains a system in existence.

2a.  **Every reasonable candidate criterion** for the property 
    (regardless of which ones are favoured by a particular theorist) is satisfied by the maintaining network
    somewhere within the composite.

2b.  **Every reasonable candidate criterion** for the property 
    (regardless of which ones are favoured by a particular theorist) is satisfied by the maintaining
    network within a specific area of the composite where the network performs some additional function(s)
    required by the criteria candidates.

3.  **Individuation via takeover.** Given such a network, determine which entity is its correct
    subject maintained by that network (i.e. that for which the takeover is complete, even if it is a hybrid entity).

Process integration (1) only shows that the disputed process belongs to some maintaining network, without
individuating the entity.
Condition (3) is what settles which entity's criteria (2a/2b) for the property of life 
are actually being tested, be it the virocell,
or any of the intermediate cases discussed below. 
Existing approaches to this same selection problem work by minimality: Krakauer et al. (2020) note that the
smallest composite meeting (1) and (2) depends on which criterion is chosen, so minimality alone does not
converge on one answer. IIT's exclusion postulate replaces size with degree, selecting the candidate system with
maximal integrated information among overlapping alternatives (Albantakis et al. 2023). Takeover 
bases individuation based on the entity identified by the process running it. 

In the case of identifying the property of having life, we note that the life-maintenance processes are,
in fact, the organism's network of metabolic processes, so the disputed process referred to in (1), (2a), and
(2b) is the maintaining network itself, and both (2a) and (2b) pick out the entire organism as a whole. 
In the virocell, the candidate network is the network of metabolic, life-maintaining
processes, which includes replicating viral DNA and maintaining the cell boundary. 
Individuation via takeover (condition (3)) is what makes a cell a virocell, 
with the network in (1) maintaining 
the very boundary around its function specified in (2a)/(2b). 

The central goal of this work is to describe any candidates for "the AI system"
that are process-integrated (1), individuated by takeover (3), and sustain computation 
(a process within the maintaining network) within them as per condition 2a or 2b. We now discuss how 
each of the following intermediate cases can be analyzed in terms of the general-form 
precedent we outline here.

### 3.4 Intermediate cases

Analysis of all these intermediate cases, like analysis of the virocell above, focuses on the property 
of having life, which happens to coincide with the network of metabolic processes. So, in the case
of life, the disputed process referred to in (1) is the maintaining network itself. 

**The wasp.**
Polydnaviruses
have been integrated into the genomes of parasitoid wasps for tens of millions of years, and viral particles are
manufactured by a small group of specialized cells in the wasp's ovary, essential to its reproduction (Burke and
Strand 2012). The network of metabolic processes extends
throughout the entire wasp, but it performs the virus replication function, satisfying the property's criteria
per (2b), only in this one tissue.
No takeover, condition (3), occurs here: the viral genes are not foreign material that has captured the
ovary cells' machinery, but a permanent, heritable part of the wasp's own genome, present in every cell
including the germline. What is regionalized is not this genetic identity but the genome's expression: only in
the ovary's calyx cells are the proviral segments excised from the chromosome, replicated into many extra
copies, expressed, then formed into viral particles. 
That the ovary cells also go on serving the
wasp's own reproduction is a coincidence of this case, not evidence of a partial takeover. 
Complete takeover is a matter
of whether the entire genome is redirected by a full replacement of the genome, 
not of whether the affected cells happen to keep doing
their other jobs.

This localization is not special to viral replication. Metabolism happens throughout the wasp, but whatever
specific job a given organ does, digestion in the gut, excretion in the malpighian tubules, is likewise carried
out only there. The ovary's viral replication function is just one more instance of this general pattern: every
organ's metabolism is put to some further, more specific use particular to that organ.

**Mitochondria.**
Descended from a free-living bacterium, mitochondria once satisfied (1) on their own: an independent cell
maintaining its own metabolism. That standing self-maintenance was not redirected to a foreign program, as in
the virocell. Instead, it was absorbed. Mitochondria retain a residual genome but depend on thousands of
nuclear-encoded proteins, imported from the cytoplasm, to build and run themselves, and they divide only on a
schedule set by the host cell cycle, so criterion (1) is now satisfied only at the level of the eukaryotic cell,
not at the level of the mitochondrion alone. Whether an isolated mitochondrion is alive poses the same kind of
problem the bare virion poses, since it can no longer survive, replicate, or metabolize on its own. The boundary
of process integration has moved outward with it: the mitochondrion's activity, specified by (2b), now
belongs to the eukaryotic cell's maintaining network. The eukaryotic cell itself is alive,
and the mitochondrion is one of its organelles.
Thus, we observe that boundary of the mitochondrion metabolism coincides with the 
boundary of the host cell, which is outside itself, but it did not repurpose the entire host cell.
Neither did the host cell replace the DNA of the mitochondrion with its own. 
As a result, complete takeover occurs here, as the boundaries for (1) and (2b) of life do not 
*all* coincide with the mitochondrial boundary.

**Artificial life.**
The same test explains why artificial life never found its virocell. Ray's Tierra creatures satisfy (2b)
on their own terms: their code is a heritable program, copied with mutation and sorted by competition for
processor time. What they lack is any analogue of (1) and (3). The hardware that runs them maintains itself,
via power delivery, cooling, and error correction, independently of which programs happen to be executing.
Maintenance is not redirected to, or constituted by, the digital organisms' replication the way a cell's
metabolism is redirected to viral propagation. There is no composite whose network of self-maintaining
processes includes the disputed process (code execution). The digital organisms behave like the virion, replicating and
evolving, but without ever finding a boundary that plays the virocell's role. This is one reason their status,
unlike the virus's, never settled by relocating the question (Bedau 2007; Koh and Ling 2023).

**Organ transplants.** 
A transplanted kidney makes a comparable point to the discussion of the wasp case. 
There, the wasp's own genome underlies both the general body and the specialized viral replication function localized
to the ovary. A transplanted kidney breaks that
continuity: its nephrons carry the donor's genome, not the recipient's, yet once grafted they resume filtration
using their own metabolism, exactly as they did in the donor, while every other maintaining process, blood
supply, immune tolerance, neural and hormonal regulation, now belongs to the recipient. The graft's
process-integration boundary is fixed by whose machinery currently sustains it, not by whose genome it carries:
rejection is a failure of that integration, and long-term graft acceptance depends
on the recipient's own regulatory processes coming to tolerate permanently foreign
machinery (Starzl and Zinkernagel 2001).




## 4. A virocell analogue for consciousness

Section 3 relocated the virus question by finding a composite in which the machinery that carries out the
disputed process, ordinarily held apart from a system's maintenance, belongs to the network of processes that
maintains the system in existence. The virocell supplied that composite for viruses. Section 2 states the corresponding requirement for
AI: a candidate subject of consciousness must be a system whose computation belongs to the network of
processes that maintains it. We can now address the question of whether anything currently called an AI has
a candidate for the virocell's role in the AI consciousness debate.

The model and the persona can be set aside quickly. Neither is a physical process at all: the model is an
abstract parameter set realized in indefinitely many copies, and the persona has no determinate physical
extent. The human brain by itself is also ruled out, 
since this gives us a model of tool use rather than joint consciousness. So is the hardware instance 
by itself, since it does not necessarily implement the model. 
The following candidates are the ones left under serious consideration.

**The hardware instance with the model installed on it.** 
The datacenter that supports the model's computation does maintain itself: power, cooling, and component
replacement keep the installation running, and it is this installation that executes inference. But computation
is not part of the network that maintains the installation. Maintenance here is serviceable independently of computation: a GPU can be swapped, cooling
capacity added or removed, or the installation idled, all without touching what is computed or being touched
by it. Process integration fails. The same absence recurs, in different guises, in every wider
composite considered below.

**The dyad.** By this, we mean the pair of the human user and the hardware instance with the model installed.
Consider first the version of the dyad available today, where the human-computer channel runs through a
keyboard, mouse, and screen. We take the brain to be the boundary of the human side of this pairing (the
embodied cognition objection is addressed in Section 6), so that the rest of the human body's role is only to
sustain the channel. Under Section 2's requisite-variety argument, a channel this coarse cannot carry what
reciprocal production and maintenance would require, and the dyad fails the bandwidth test outright.

A second, independent argument does not even need bandwidth. The human who maintains the hardware, paying for
power, replacing failed components, restarting a crashed process, is typically not the same human interacting
with the model in any given conversation. Reciprocal production and maintenance, in Section 2's sense, needs a
single locus whose activity both sustains and is sustained by the same network. Splitting that locus across two
different humans, one who keeps the machine running and one who talks to it, means neither side's persistence
is produced by the other's activity even in principle, independently of how the channel between them is built.

Two further worries turn on the same variable: how low a bandwidth the criterion can tolerate before it fails.
One aggregates the connection across many agents rather than concentrating it in a single dyad, asking whether
an appropriate candidate can be built from multiple hardware/software and human agents together. We address
this possibility as both a discussion of such a candidate below, and in an objection in Section 6. The other
raises bandwidth from the opposite direction, asking whether a single dyad's connection could simply be made
dense enough to close the gap outright; that question is developed on its own in the discussion of
human-computer integration in Section 5.

A further refinement bears on how firm this verdict is against future engineering. Section 2 gives the general
test for what would count as closing the dyad's gap: not bandwidth, which invites the reply that a shovel and a
neurally interfaced prosthetic differ only in degree of coupling, but reciprocal production and maintenance
within a single constraint network. By that test the hardware instance fails because its upkeep is produced by
processes external to what it computes. The dyad fails because neither side's persistence is produced by
the other's activity, however much bandwidth a future interface supplied. 

**The human mind, for the AI's content.** A further reading of the human-brain case changes the subject rather
than answering it. If the relevant process is taken to be the AI's content rather than its computation, the
user's brain does execute that content with its own maintaining machinery, in which case the human mind, not
any candidate object built from silicon, plays the virocell's role, for the AI's content if not for the AI's
computation. This "parasitic" reading is taken up in Section 7. It suggests that the question "is the AI
conscious" is misdirected in a further way: fixing an object via a consciousness boundary, 
as Section 2 asks us to, still leaves us to specify its inseparable 
process that is responsible for both computation and maintenance.

**Society, hardware, and software.** The same test extends to the broader candidates suggested by (viii) in
Section 2: the community of human trainers whose judgments shaped the model's parameters, and the staff who
maintain its datacenters, each considered collectively rather than as individuals. Both fail the bandwidth
test more decisively than the dyad does, since what connects a trainer's labeling decision, or a technician's
maintenance ticket, to the model's ongoing computation is mediated through code review, deployment pipelines,
and institutional process rather than any direct channel at all. Neither aggregate reciprocally produces or is
produced by the model's computation in the sense Section 2 requires. This does not close the question whether
such an aggregate could constitute a new agent in its own right, distinct from any of its members, the way a
corporation's goals belong to no individual officer. That possibility is real,
and it is taken up directly as an objection in Section 6.

**Other computing substrates.** Three further substrates raise the process-integration question in a different
key, and are addressed separately because none is what is ordinarily meant by "an AI." The verdict below
concerns the paradigm case of a trained model running on conventional hardware; these substrates are exceptions
to it, not further instances of it.

*Lab-grown neural tissue.* Cultured human neurons, whether dissociated cortical cultures wired to a
multielectrode array (Kagan et al. 2022) or the organoid-based "biocomputing" trajectory now under development
(Smirnova et al. 2023), are kept alive by the same perfusion and gas-exchange system that also carries their
electrical signaling to and from the array; unlike the datacenter's power and cooling, this maintenance cannot
be swapped out without touching what is computed. The point generalizes beyond this particular substrate:
reconsidering the classic brain-in-a-vat thought experiment, Thompson and Cosmelli (2011) argue that any vat
adequate to keep a brain conscious would have to reproduce a living body's own physiological support in full,
collapsing the contrast the thought experiment relies on between an isolated brain and its body. Process
integration, unlike in the hardware-instance case, is not obviously absent here, and the bioethics literature on
cerebral organoids already treats their possible sentience as a live question in its own right (Lavazza and
Massimini 2018; Niikawa et al. 2022). What remains
unclear is scale: whether many such cultures, wired together as nodes of a single facility, communicate with
enough bandwidth to constitute one computation process rather than many separate ones is exactly Section 2's
requisite-variety question, and it is unresolved for this substrate; the answer plausibly turns on the same
bandwidth variable at stake for the dyad, rather than on any consideration specific to neural tissue. The
metabolic boundary, however, is not in doubt: each culture's own perfusion system, not the facility housing it,
is where its maintenance is drawn.

*Synthetic cell computation.* Genetic circuits engineered into living cells to perform Boolean logic (Xiang,
Dalchau and Wang 2018) raise the same question from the opposite side of the hardware case. A circuit's
transcription and translation draw on the same finite pool of ribosomes, RNA polymerase, and metabolic
resources as the cell's own housekeeping genes, a competition documented under the name of cellular "burden":
added circuit complexity measurably slows growth, and a cell's finite capacity measurably constrains what the
circuit can express (Ceroni et al. 2015). Maintenance and computation cannot be swapped out independently of
each other here any more than in the neural-tissue case, which makes process integration a live candidate in a
way it is not for silicon hardware. Whether a bacterium executing a genetic logic gate is a candidate "AI" in
any sense relevant to this debate is a separate question this paper does not settle.

*Neural implants.* An implant is not a further computing substrate in this sense at all, but a channel joining
two substrates already on the list, human neural tissue and hardware, and so raises no new process-integration
question of its own; it is treated under the dyad above and, at greater length, in Section 5.

**Verdict.**
Across every candidate built from a trained model on conventional hardware, the necessary condition fails. This
failure is itself the finding, not a gap in the paper's ability to settle the question. Section 3 introduces a
prerequisite for settling the AI consciousness dispute: there must exist a composite whose network of maintaining processes includes
computation. Unlike the virus debate, which had a determinate
answer waiting at the virocell, there is no structurally appropriate composite onto which to relocate the AI
debate. Moreover, the takeover criterion was not tested on any of the proposed candidates, as it only makes
sense to apply once computation is found to belong to a maintaining network. Section 3's sufficient condition,
that a qualifying composite exhibit every property treated as criterial, goes untested for the same reason: it
too presupposes a composite that clears the necessary condition first, and none does. The other substrates
addressed above, lab-grown neural tissue and synthetic cell computation, are not covered by this verdict:
process integration is not obviously absent for either, and whether either counts as "AI" in the sense this
debate concerns is itself unsettled.

## 5. Human-computer integration

Section 4 set aside, for separate treatment, a more hypothetical version of the dyad: an interface dense
enough to close the bandwidth gap, and to place maintenance responsibility on the same human who does the
communicating, rather than split across a user and a separate maintenance staff. Research aimed at narrowing
the bandwidth gap specifically is already underway. High-bandwidth speech neuroprostheses now decode intended
speech at rates approaching natural conversation (Willett et al. 2023), and non-invasive brain-to-brain
interfaces have linked multiple subjects directly (Jiang et al. 2019). Nothing in the criterion defended here
rules out, in principle, an interface of this kind meeting it. Reciprocal production and maintenance is a
necessary structural property of a candidate system (Section 2),
so a sufficiently integrated human-computer composite may qualify regardless of
how the connection is physically implemented. This is also the one case in the paper where the takeover
condition's asymmetry is not idle: the human side of the dyad is uncontroversially conscious antecedently, while
the model's status is exactly what is in dispute, so which party's machinery is described as doing the
redirecting matters to the verdict.

Should such an interface exist, more than one attitude toward the result is defensible, and we do not
adjudicate between them here. 
On one reading, since the human side of the dyad
plausibly already satisfies the criterion on its own (Section 2), a fused composite adds nothing to what was
already true of the human, and the question that motivated the inquiry, whether the model's own computation
is what is conscious, remains as open after process integration as before it. 
Another reading follows the relocation-then-deflation pattern of Section 3 directly:
the resulting subject would not be the AI, but a new composite, a human-computer individual, whose
consciousness belongs to that composite rather than to the model or the installation taken alone.
This mirrors how 
the virocell's aliveness belongs to the infected cell rather than to the virion. On this reading, the interface
would supply a virocell of sorts, which would adjudicate the consciousness question for a different 
entity than the one originally in dispute. 
On yet another reading, the consciousness boundary of this "virocell analog" is already the relevant subject 
for the AI consciousness debate, rather than a relocation. Here, we must adjudicate whether the process 
integration and takeover conditions are met. The conclusion here hinges significantly on the 
exact specification of such a future entity, but the interfaces described here (or, at least sufficiently 
advanced forms thereof) exist only as research trajectories today.

## 6. Objections

### Criterion

One objection reads the criterion as a claim about temporal continuity: a system whose activity can be
suspended and resumed should not count as conscious, since experience cannot survive gaps. Anaesthesia shows
why this fails. Under general anaesthesia much cortical computation continues, yet experience is lost, and
what tracks the loss is the collapse of integration rather than any interruption of processing. The relevant
facts are facts about organization, and continuity is a poor proxy for them. The criterion is accordingly
organizational, not temporal: it is not a constraint on the schedule of a system's operation.

A related worry is that an anaesthetized animal cannot feed itself, evade threats, or otherwise act to secure
its own survival, and that this looks like exactly the kind of breakdown the criterion should register. But
the criterion tracks the identity of the processes that maintain the organism and the processes that compute,
not the organism's behavioral capacity to act on its environment. While unconscious, an animal loses the
latter but not the former: respiration, circulation, and cellular repair continue uninterrupted, carried out
by the same physiological processes that resume computing when the animal wakes. What lapses is provisioning,
not organization, and provisioning is already excluded from the criterion, as the discussion of autonomy of
supply below makes explicit.

A second objection reduces the criterion to embodiment, vulnerability, or autonomy of energy supply, and with
it disputes where within a person the resulting boundary should even be drawn. Man and Damasio (2019) propose
robots endowed with homeostatic needs as a route to machine feeling. Whatever such needs add, the resulting
architecture retains a seam: a homeostatic subsystem coupled to a computational core through a defined
interface, with either component replaceable without touching the other. A more demanding case is an embodied
robot that secures its own energy and raw materials. Such a robot is self-maintaining in the engineering sense,
yet its goals sit in addressable memory and remain reprogrammable, by itself or by others, because nothing in
its constitution forces them on it. An animal cannot rewrite hunger. Provisioning is not process integration, and autonomy
of supply is not inseparability.

The bandwidth criterion invites its own contested examples. The human body as a whole, and the brain within
it, are both reasonable candidates for a person's own consciousness boundary, and the criterion applies to
each differently. The body as a whole is itself a densely coupled homeostatic network, with circulation,
respiration, and repair each sustaining and sustained by the others. It is therefore 
an obvious candidate for the
organism's own boundary of process integration. Like the brain, it is also an information processing and
communication network of continuous causal coupling. Unlike the brain, on the other hand, it is
not coupled to itself as densely, or at every scale, as the brain is internally. Hence it is a
contested candidate for the consciousness boundary. Embodied cognition, in the form defended by Varela,
Thompson, and Rosch (1991), holds that experience is not implemented by neural computation alone but
constitutively shaped by sensorimotor and physiological processes distributed through the body: interoceptive
signaling, the visceral systems that inform affect, and the perception-action loops that structure what is
perceived in the first place. On this view, the boundary of experience should not
be fixed at the skull. We do not need to settle this dispute here: the body's coupling, however much sparser
than the brain's, remains continuous and causal, the same kind of coupling our criterion was built to track,
so a graded verdict is at least available. The composites considered for AI fail even this weaker test.
Section 2 treats reciprocal production and maintenance as present or absent, not a matter of degree, and the
channels connecting these composites, coarse and serial rather than dense and parallel, fall well short of the
bandwidth that relation requires (Section 2's requisite-variety argument). The difference is one of kind, not
degree, and embodied cognition's residual-coupling argument, whatever its merits for the body, gives no
foothold for extending consciousness to the human-computer pairing.

A third objection asks why the criterion does not also grant process integration to composites bridged by feedback, such
as a human coupled to a computer, or two humans in sustained conversation, since information flows in both
directions and each shapes the other's ongoing activity. Section 2 gives the general answer, reciprocal
production and maintenance within a single constraint network, not bandwidth, and Section 4 applies it to the
dyad directly: by that test no dyad yet built qualifies.

### Virocell

The following objection targets the dyad analysis directly: low-bandwidth aggregation can support a genuine
emergent goal, as when a corporation's pursuit of market share is not the goal of any individual officer yet
emerges reliably from thin channels of memos, votes, and contracts (List and Pettit 2011), and one might expect
the same aggregation to meet our process integration criterion.

The literature on group minds gives the general reply: goal-directed organization and process integration, on
our criterion, come apart. Thin channels have long been
recognized as adequate to the former. Block's (1978) China Brain
already turns on this gap, since a population linked only by phone calls can implement any functional
organization one likes without any member's own maintaining processes being redirected to the group's
computation. Schwitzgebel
(2015) presses the same structure from the other side, arguing that a committed functionalist should accept
the United States as a candidate for conscious organism,
and treats this as a cost his opponents must pay rather than an independently
plausible verdict. Let us grant, for the sake of argument, that a corporation's survival, orchestrated by its
sparsely connected members, is coordinated closely enough to resemble the network of maintaining processes, computation
included, that our criterion asks after. Granting this does not by itself grant process integration: takeover, in Section 3's
sense, asks whether the redirection of machinery is total, and here it is not. The corporation's persistence
does not reciprocally produce and sustain the persistence of the individual humans who compose it. Each
continues to eat, rest, and pursue ends the corporation never set, maintained by processes the corporation's
own activity does not touch. The same holds for an AI system that comes to exercise broad influence over human
affairs: influence of this kind changes what the humans around it do, not what maintains them, so it does not
amount to takeover in Section 3's sense either.

This leaves room for a further possibility, though, one the human-computer integration discussion in Section 5
already anticipated for the individual case. An aggregate whose members' own maintaining processes were genuinely redirected,
rather than merely correlated with its persistence, would meet the criterion regardless of how coarse the
channel connecting them was, once redirection was total. Nothing said here rules this out in principle. But
nothing in the arrangements between any AI system and the humans who train, deploy, or work alongside it shows
that redirection has occurred. Should this change, the finding above would not be overturned so much as
relocated, in the same sense Section 3's deflationary move relocated the virus question (§3): the entity found
conscious, if any were, would be the new composite, not "the AI" in the sense the original question intended.
