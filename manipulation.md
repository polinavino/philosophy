# Manipulation as Cost-Imposition: An Addendum to the Causal-Transparency Account

## Abstract

The causal-transparency account of free speech [1] treats Types 1 and 2 of the Lowe taxonomy
as background conditions rather than sites of analysis. This addendum argues that manipulation
can operate at all four levels. We define manipulation as the introduction of obstacles to a target's capacity to formulate,
express, or act on a view, which are obstacles not present before the interaction and that serve the
manipulator's ends rather than the target's. A necessary condition we introduce specificity: the obstacles
must attach to the act of maintaining the view in particular, not to the interaction as a whole.
The obstacle takes a different form at each level: corrupted belief formation at Type 0, removed
expressive capacity at Type 1, severed communicative channel at Type 2, and raised cost of
expression at Type 3.

We show that manipulation-occurrence, free speech status, and culpability are three independent
conditions that must be tracked separately, illustrating this with examples.
We extend the account to lying as a form of
manipulation that operates through the epistemic environment rather than the relational one, and
show that it is exclusively a Type 0 phenomenon.

We survey the principal contexts in which the mechanism appears, including emotional manipulation,
gaslighting, propaganda, coercive control, official language control, channel control, and
commercial manipulation, and show that each is an instance of the same structural account.
We then develop a diagnostic. The primary criterion is whether the target's capacity to speak
freely on the affected topic has been undermined: successful manipulation always produces this
effect. Because this criterion is harder to apply at deeper levels (the effect is unfelt at
Type 0 and invisible to the target at Types 1 and 2) we introduce intent as a complementary
diagnostic: evidence that obstacles were specifically designed to track resistance to a
particular view is evidence that the mechanism is operative, even when its effect cannot be
directly observed.

## 1 Introduction

The causal-transparency account [1] analyses free speech as a property of the space of possible
utterances available to a speaker on a topic in a context: an utterance is freely spoken to the
degree that the causal pathway from the speaker's communicative intentions to the utterance is
recoverable by a competent interlocutor, and the cost to the speaker of making this pathway
transparent is low. Type 3 speech constraints raise this cost — through censorship, social cost,
coercion, or chilling effects. Type 0 names a background condition: obstructions at the level of
thought-formation that are upstream of the analysis in [1], framing its outer limit.

The account characterises, within a given
communicative situation, what free speech consists of and what undermines it. It treats Types 1 and 2 as background conditions: the speaker is
assumed to have physical capacity and a functional channel, rather than as sites where the
analysis applies. This addendum argues that this restriction is not principled: manipulation can
operate at all four levels of the taxonomy. A government that controls official language
manipulates at Type 1, removing the expressive capacity to formulate certain views. A government
that controls communicative infrastructure manipulates at Type 2, severing or filtering channels
before any question of cost on expression arises. These are not edge cases but among the most
consequential and historically attested forms of manipulation. 

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Philosophical Accounts of Manipulation

The dominant account in the philosophy of action treats manipulation as influence that bypasses
rational agency. Rudinow [2] offers an early formal treatment, characterising manipulation as
influence through illegitimate means that circumvent rather than engage the target's deliberative
capacities. Noggle [3] refines this as influence that distorts or exploits non-rational
psychological mechanisms — emotional states, biases, identity — rather than providing reasons the
target could evaluate. Allen Wood [4] gives the most explicitly normative version: manipulation
works through means the agent would repudiate if they understood them, placing the wrong in the
use of mechanisms the target is not positioned to assess or contest.

The comprehensive treatment in Coons and Weber [5] identifies a persistent difficulty across
these accounts: the boundary between rational and non-rational influence is not stable. Emotional
appeals, rhetorical salience, and framing effects are not straightforwardly separable from
reason-giving, and a purely mechanism-based account risks either over-inclusiveness (any
emotional influence becomes manipulation) or under-inclusiveness (only deception counts).
The present paper addresses this difficulty with the specificity condition: it is not
non-rational influence as such that is manipulative, but influence that specifically attaches its
costs to the act of maintaining a position.

### 2.2 Propaganda and Mass Manipulation

Ellul [6] distinguishes two structural types of propaganda that map onto the Type 3/Type 0
distinction. Agitation propaganda targets action: it raises the cost of inaction or dissent in
the short term, producing behaviour change while leaving the underlying belief structure
partially intact — a Type 3 effect. Integration propaganda operates over longer timescales,
aiming at the absorption of a worldview rather than a specific behaviour. It produces belief
change whose origin in the mechanism is invisible to the subject -— a Type 0 effect. Ellul's
central claim, that propaganda operates most effectively when the target does not recognise it as
such, is the mass-scale analogue of the diagnostic developed in section 6: Type 0 effects are
unfelt and visible only from outside.

Stanley [7] analyses propaganda as the deployment of speech acts that corrupt the epistemic
environment in which political deliberation occurs, making certain conclusions appear more
evidenced than they are. His account focuses on the distortion of the information environment
rather than the emotional cost structure, and connects more directly to the lying case discussed
in section 3.3. Arendt [8] examines the totalitarian case as one in which the mechanism is
institutionalised: the cost structure is not introduced by individual interactions but built into
the social and political environment, making it a standing feature of every topic-context cell.

### 2.3 Gaslighting and Epistemic Manipulation

Abramson [9] gives the most rigorous philosophical treatment of gaslighting as a distinct
manipulative form. On her account, gaslighting does not merely impose costs on the target's
capacity to maintain a view. It targets the target's confidence in their own perceptual and
epistemic reliability. This is a Type 0 effect operating on a beliefs-formation level : the target's ability
to form and hold object-level beliefs is undermined, not by changing any particular belief but
by corrupting the process through which beliefs are assessed as credible at all. The target
cannot identify the manipulation because the instrument through which they would identify it —-
their trust in their own perception —- has itself been manipulated. Fricker's analysis of
testimonial injustice [10] addresses a structurally related mechanism at the social level:
when a speaker's credibility is systematically deflated by identity prejudice, the cost of
maintaining their testimony as true is imposed by the social context rather than by a
particular interlocutor.

### 2.4 Coercive Control

Stark [11] documents coercive control in intimate partner relationships as a systematic pattern
of manipulation that operates cumulatively, combining Type 3 and Type 0 effects over time. No
single interaction need constitute manipulation: what is manipulative is the architecture of
costs imposed across interactions, which progressively degrades the target's capacity to hold,
express, or act on their own views. The mechanism can therefore be distributed across interactions rather than concentrated in a
single act, and the diagnostic applies at whichever level the pattern operates.

### 2.5 Behavioral Influence and Commercial Manipulation

Thaler and Sunstein [12] introduce nudging as influence that alters choice architecture without
restricting options or changing incentives. Their account is explicitly non-manipulative on its
own framing: nudges exploit predictable biases but in ways the designer would endorse if the
target were aware of them. The present account identifies the manipulative limit of nudging
precisely: a nudge becomes manipulation when the cost it imposes on a particular option
specifically tracks the target's attempt to maintain that option, rather than being a uniform
feature of the choice environment. Cialdini [13] catalogues the psychological mechanisms through
which commercial influence operates -— reciprocity, commitment, social proof, authority, scarcity
—- each of which is a version of cost-imposition on resistance, exploiting the target's own
cognitive and social tendencies to raise the cost of non-compliance.

## 3 The Cost-Imposition Account

### 3.1 The Mechanism

An interaction constitutes manipulation of target T by agent A if and only if 
(1) A's conduct
introduces obstacles to T's capacity to hold, express, or act on a view V in a topic-context cell
(Tv, C) obstacles that were not present in (Tv, C) prior to A's conduct, and 
(2) these obstacles
attach specifically to the act of maintaining V, rather than being uniformly distributed across
T's behaviour in C.

The form of the obstacle varies by level. At Type 0, the obstacle corrupts belief formation:
T's epistemic process is shaped so that V is not arrived at or not retained. At Type 1, the
obstacle removes expressive capacity: T lacks the linguistic tools to produce an
utterance that maintains V. At Type 2, the obstacle severs the channel: T can formulate the
utterance but cannot reach the relevant interlocutor. At Type 3, the obstacle is a cost on
expression: T retains capacity and channel but faces adverse consequences for using them to
maintain V. At Types 0, 1, and 2 the obstacle is structural: the capacity, belief-forming
process, or channel is absent or corrupted rather than merely expensive to use. At Type 3 the
cost is finite and T chooses not to pay it. Note that the Type 3 case raises the question of genuine
emotion and its manipulative capacity. This is addressed in section 3.4.

The "not present before" qualifier distinguishes manipulation from reporting. Informing T that
holding V carries social costs that already exist in the world does not manipulate: it does not
introduce obstacles, it highlights them. What manipulation introduces are new obstacles, whether
costs, corrupted inputs, removed capacity, or severed channels, through A's conduct rather than
through any pre-existing feature of T's situation.

The specificity condition that "obstacles attach to maintaining V rather than being distributed
uniformly" distinguishes manipulation from difficult interactions generally. The manipulative
structure is one in which the obstacle intensifies when T expresses or maintains V, and eases
when T yields. This structural signature applies across all four levels, but may be hard to 
identify if the targe succumbs to (what could be) manipulation immediately.  

### 3.2 Manipulation Is Not Persuasion

Persuasion operates by providing reasons that bear on whether V is correct or desirable. The
target's belief change, if any, tracks the reasons: T would have changed their view given the
same reasons in the absence of any additional cost on maintaining V. Manipulation operates by
raising the cost of maintaining V regardless of whether such reasons are supplied. The diagnostic
is counterfactual: if T would have changed their view given the same information, absent the
cost-imposition, the influence is persuasion. If not, the mechanism is manipulative even if the
resulting belief is now sincerely held.

Genuine belief change does not dissolve manipulation. A target who now sincerely holds the
revised view may have arrived there through cost-tracking rather than reason-tracking. The
authenticity of the resulting belief does not bear on how it was produced. Similarly, the
authenticity of A's emotional expression is irrelevant to whether manipulation is occurring. A
genuine emotion that imposes costs specifically on T's capacity to maintain V is manipulative by
mechanism, whether or not it is sincere.

A salesperson example illustrates the persuasion-manipulation boundary and introduces a further
case. When a buyer has specific articulated objections, e.g. price, quality, delivery time, and the
salesperson responds with relevant information or revised terms, this is persuasion: the
mechanism engages the reasons behind the view, not the capacity to hold it. The case changes
when the salesperson specifically targets the buyer's capacity to maintain resistance. A buyer
who cannot formulate why they do not want to buy, or who is apprehensive about asserting "I
don't want this," holds a pre-articulate view, a "felt reluctance", whose expression is already
fragile. A salesperson who exploits this by sustaining the interaction past the point of natural
exit, by making assertion of disinterest feel socially costly, or by keeping the conversation
moving so that resistance cannot crystallise into a clear statement, is imposing an obstacle on
the act of maintaining V. The inability to formulate the resistance is not a reason-gap that
legitimate persuasion should address. Rather, it is a signal that the target's free speech capacity on
the topic is already under pressure. The endpoint is diagnostic: a buyer who leaves having
purchased something they did not want, and who could not have freely said "I don't want to" at
any point in the interaction, has had their free speech capacity on the topic undermined —
whether or not the salesperson intended this.

### 3.3 Lying as Manipulation

Lying is excluded from free speech in [1] on the grounds that the causal pathway from the
speaker's communicative intentions to their utterance is made opaque by design. Here we show
that lying also constitutes manipulation, and that it operates on the same cost-imposition
mechanism as emotional manipulation, but via the epistemic environment rather than the relational
one.

A lie induces a false belief B in T. Given B, certain options become more or less costly than
they would under accurate conditions: the target's assessment of the costs and benefits of
available actions is computed against a corrupted input. The lie does not directly raise the cost
of maintaining V. Rather, it distorts T's model of what V costs, or distorts T's model of the situation
such that V is no longer identified as the relevant view to hold. The cost-imposition is therefore
one level removed. 

Two structural features distinguish lying manipulation from emotional manipulation. First, lying
is always intentional: as established in [1], a lie requires the deliberate engineering of
opacity, which is an authored act. This means lying manipulation always carries culpability on
the part of A, in contrast to emotional manipulation, which may occur without culpability when
the expression is genuinely involuntary. Second, lying manipulation is exclusively a Type 0
phenomenon. The false belief induced by the lie replaces the belief T would have held under
accurate conditions, T's subsequent speech flows freely from the resulting (corrupted) belief
state, with no felt constraint.

### 3.4 Other Categories of Manipulation

The cost-imposition mechanism takes different forms across contexts, varying in medium, scale,
and the ratio of Type 0 to Type 3 effects.

**Emotional manipulation** is the paradigm case addressed in section 3.1. Costs are imposed
through the emotional and relational consequences of A's expression, and both Type 3 and Type 0
effects are possible depending on whether the target's belief changes or only their expression.
Love-bombing, guilt-tripping, and threat of emotional withdrawal are instances, distinguished by
which specific cost (e.g. relational loss, identity threat, or anticipated distress) is targeted.

A manipulator expressing genuine emotion may appear to be exposing an obstacle that already
exists, as the hurt and the relationship damage is real. The relevant test is not whether the
emotion is sincere or proportionate to the situation, but whether its expression specifically
tracks T's resistance: intensifying when T maintains V and easing when T yields. If so, the
specificity condition is met and manipulation is occurring regardless of the emotion's
authenticity or proportionality. Both bear on culpability, not on occurrence.

**Gaslighting** operates primarily at Type 0, but its target is not an object-level view V so
much as T's epistemic confidence in their own capacity to assess V. The costs are imposed on
T's act of trusting their own perceptions, rather than on any particular conclusion those
perceptions support. The diagnostic in section 6 applies, but the free speech capacity that is
undermined is more general: it is T's capacity to speak from their own epistemic standpoint on
any topic touched by the gaslighting, not only the topic in dispute.

**Propaganda** operates at mass scale, distributing cost-imposition across a population rather
than between individuals. In agitation propaganda (Type 3 dominant), the cost of dissenting
behaviour is raised through social and institutional mechanisms. In integration propaganda (Type
0 dominant), the belief formation environment is shaped over time so that certain conclusions
come to feel natural and others feel evidentially unjustified. The manipulator in the mass-scale
case is an institution or system rather than an individual. The mechanism is the same, but culpability
is distributed and therefore harder to locate.

**Coercive control** is the cumulative case. No single interaction constitutes a complete
manipulation. The cost structure is built up across a pattern of interactions that progressively
degrades T's capacity to hold or express their own views. Both Type 3 and Type 0 effects
accumulate: early interactions may produce only behavioural compliance (Type 3) while later ones
produce genuine belief revision (Type 0) as T's model of their own situation is reorganised
around the cost structure the relationship has imposed.

**Commercial manipulation and dark patterns** exploit cognitive tendencies identified in the
behavioral influence literature (section 2.5). The specificity condition applies here. Consider
a scarcity signal or social proof indicator. If it applies uniformly across all choices, it is
a feature of the choice environment, and therefore not manipulation. If it is engineered to specifically
raise the cost of one outcome, i.e. declining or not complying, it is manipulative. The
difference is whether the obstacle attaches to a particular observable act of resistance or to the
situation as a whole. Examples include: a checkout button that is hard to find is an inconvenience 
or a cancellation
flow designed to be harder to complete than the original sign-up is manipulative.

**Manipulative questions and framing** impose costs on the speech act itself through
presupposition. A question that takes as given something T would dispute, but for which
contesting the presupposition requires a costly meta-level intervention, is a cost-imposition
built into the structure of the question. The cost structure is not introduced by emotional
expression but by the formal properties of the framing: certain responses are much cheaper than
others not because they are more accurate but because the question's presuppositions have been
designed to make challenge expensive.

### 3.5 Manipulation at Types 1 and 2

The cases above operate primarily at Types 3 and 0. Manipulation can also operate at Types 1
and 2, where the obstacle is not a cost on an available capacity but the removal of capacity or
channel itself.

**Type 1 manipulation — capacity control.** A government that mandates official vocabulary,
restricts permissible terminology in law or education, or systematically excludes minority
language from institutional discourse removes the linguistic tools through which
certain views can be formulated and expressed. The free-speech paper notes that state-mandated
vocabulary restrictions sit at the intersection of Types 0 and 2, because their intended effect
is to constrain what the speaker can think and express. The present account is more precise:
Type 1 manipulation targets the production capacity directly: the speaker may have the
underlying concept but no socially available or legally sanctioned form in which to utter it.
Orwell's Newspeak is the limiting case: a vocabulary engineered so that the concepts required
to formulate dissenting views literally cannot be expressed, with the further aim of making them
unthinkable (a Type 0 effect downstream of the Type 1 intervention). The relationship between
Type 1 and Type 0 is therefore frequently one of means to end: capacity control is the
mechanism, belief-formation corruption is the intended outcome.

**Type 2 manipulation — channel control.** A state that monopolises media, filters information
infrastructure, or controls which voices reach which audiences manipulates at Type 2. The
obstacle here is not the cost of expression within an existing channel but the substitution of
a controlled channel for an open one, or the selective severing of channels that would carry
particular content. Unlike Type 3 censorship, which raises the cost of transparency while
leaving the channel technically open, Type 2 manipulation closes the channel, making the
question of expression cost moot: the utterance can be produced but cannot reach its intended
interlocutor. Type 2 manipulation is also distinctive in operating simultaneously on the
target's incoming and outgoing information: the same infrastructure that prevents T from
reaching certain interlocutors determines what information enters T's epistemic environment,
creating the conditions for downstream Type 0 manipulation by controlling the inputs to belief
formation.

The specificity condition applies at Types 1 and 2 in the same way as at Types 3 and 0: the
manipulation is identified by whether the capacity removal or channel control attaches
specifically to the views or voices A wishes to suppress, rather than being applied uniformly.
A blanket communication blackout is a different kind of harm from a targeted restriction on
specific vocabulary or specific channels carrying specific content. The latter has the
structural signature of manipulation, while the former may be coercion, censorship, or something
else. The required specificity that marks manipulation as such (i.e. obstacles that track resistance to
a particular view) cannot be demonstrated.

## 4 Three Axes: Free Speech, Culpability, and Manipulation-Occurrence

Manipulation-occurrence, free speech status, and culpability are three genuinely separate
conditions. The earlier presentation of this account suggested that free speech and culpability
could be collapsed into a single axis. The lying case shows this is wrong. The three conditions
must be tracked independently.

### 4.1 Two Conditions

An expression is free speech, in the sense of [1], when the causal pathway from the speaker's
communicative intentions to the utterance is recoverable by a competent interlocutor.

An agent is culpable for an expression when that expression was produced by a mechanism they
owned: a reasons-responsive causal process that is appropriately attributed to them.

These are separate conditions. The author of a lie is always culpable because they controlled
the mechanism
that produced it, but a lie is never free speech, because the pathway from intention to
utterance is not recoverable. Culpability and free speech status come apart.

### 4.2 Lying as the Demonstrative Case

Lying is the clearest case of the separation. The liar owns the mechanism — the decision to
deceive is a reasons-responsive act appropriately attributed to them, so they are culpable. But
the pathway from their communicative intentions to the utterance is made unrecoverable by design,
so the lie is not free speech. Culpability attaches to the act of engineering opacity, so the
utterance produced by that act falls outside the free speech region.

This also means that free speech implies culpability, as a one-way relation: if the pathway is
recoverable, the expression was produced by the agent's communicative intentions, and they own
that mechanism. But culpability does not imply free speech, for which lying is the counterexample.

### 4.3 Implications for Manipulation

Since the three axes are genuinely independent, manipulation-occurrence is orthogonal to both
free speech status and culpability separately. The mechanism can be running regardless of where
A's expression falls on either axis.

What varies across the axes is not whether manipulation is occurring but who bears responsibility
and what kind of response is appropriate. Dispositional manipulation, where A's expression is free
speech, is an act for which A is culpable, and calls for a response directed at A. 
Involuntary manipulation, where A's
expression is not free speech, is an act for which A is not culpable. Therefore, 
it requires the mechanism to first be made visible,
but does not support the same response toward A. Lying manipulation is, again, an act for which 
A is culpable, but the
expression is not free speech. This is a third configuration, in which culpability attaches to the
act of deception rather than the content of any utterance.

The three axes serve different purposes. Manipulation-occurrence determines what T needs:
whether the mechanism is running, and therefore whether T's free speech capacity requires
protection or restoration. The free speech axis determines what protections apply to A's
expression. The culpability axis determines what response toward A is warranted. A complete
analysis of any manipulation case requires all three.

## 5 Effects at Each Level

Successful manipulation leaves a trace at whichever level of the Lowe taxonomy the obstacle was
introduced. The four effects are ordered by depth: Type 3 is the most surface-level and most
recoverable, Type 0 is the most upstream and least recoverable, and Types 1 and 2 are structural
interventions that remove the preconditions for expression rather than raising its cost.

**Type 0 effect.** T's belief regarding V changes, but the change tracks the cost of maintaining
V rather than reasons bearing on V's truth or desirability. The causal-transparency apparatus is
preserved: T speaks freely from the resulting belief, with no felt constraint. But what that
belief is was determined by the mechanism rather than by reasoning. The free speech apparatus
operates on a compromised substrate. Lying manipulation is a special case: the false belief is
induced through distortion of the epistemic environment rather than the relational one, but the
structure is the same. Type 0 effects are more difficult to recover from than Type 3: the
original belief is no longer available as a resource for resistance, and the mechanism is visible,
if at all, only from outside.

**Type 1 effect.** T lacks the expressive capacity to produce an utterance that maintains V. T
may hold the underlying view but have no sanctioned linguistic form in which to utter it. Unlike
Type 3, the gap is not felt: the absence of vocabulary for a concept can make the concept itself
harder to sustain, quietly pushing the effect toward Type 0.

**Type 2 effect.** T can formulate the utterance but cannot reach the intended interlocutor. The
channel is severed or substituted before any question of expression cost arises. Because the same
infrastructure controls incoming information as well as outgoing expression, Type 2 effects
frequently create the conditions for downstream Type 0 manipulation.

**Type 3 effect.** T's belief regarding V remains intact, but the cost of expressing or acting on
it has been raised to the point where T yields behaviourally without belief revision. The causal
pathway from T's communicative intention to T's utterance is severed or deformed by the imposed
cost structure. T is aware, at some level, of the gap between their belief and their expression.
This is the most recoverable effect: the belief is intact and the constraint is felt, so T has
resources with which to identify and contest it.

## 6 The Diagnostic

A consequence of the account is that successful manipulation always undermines the target's
capacity to speak freely on the affected topic, but the level at which that capacity is
undermined varies, and so does the tractability of identifying it.

At Type 3, the constraint is felt: T knows their belief and knows they are not expressing it.
The gap is visible to T and potentially to an interlocutor, making the manipulation the most
identifiable and most contestable. At Type 0, the constraint is unfelt: T speaks freely from
a belief that was produced by the mechanism. The only reliable evidence is external: an
observer who can compare T's prior and posterior belief-forming processes, or observe that the
belief change tracks cost rather than reasons. At Type 1, T may not be aware that a capacity
has been removed: the absence of vocabulary for a concept does not announce itself as an
absence. At Type 2, T can speak but is systematically not heard by the interlocutors whose
uptake would matter. This failure may not be visible to T from within the interaction.

In precisely these cases (where the effect on free speech capacity cannot be directly observed
by T, and in some cases by any interlocutor) intent provides a second, complementary
diagnostic. The primary diagnostic runs backward from effect: observed undermining of free
speech capacity is evidence that manipulation succeeded. The intent diagnostic runs forward from
design: evidence that A's conduct was specifically designed to introduce obstacles to T's
capacity to maintain V is evidence that the mechanism is operative, even when the effect cannot
yet be independently confirmed.

The intent diagnostic is useful because the specificity condition.
is precisely what deliberate design
produces, and what unintentional effects tend not to. A vocabulary system engineered to make
certain concepts inexpressible will show the specificity signature: the restricted terms track
particular views rather than being applied neutrally. A channel system designed to suppress
specific content will show the same. Design intent is therefore evidence of specificity, which
is the defining mark of manipulation at any level.

For Type 1 and Type 2 manipulation, the relevant intent is often not located in an individual
but distributed across an institution, such as the design intent of a vocabulary policy, a media
licensing framework, or an algorithmic filtering system. This is a weaker form of intent than
individual psychological intent, but it serves the same diagnostic function: if the system was
designed to specifically obstruct certain views, this is evidence of the mechanism regardless of
whether any individual consciously intended the full effect on any particular target.

The intent diagnostic is not available in all cases. When the manipulator is unconscious of
their own pattern, e.g.the case discussed in section 4.3, detectable design intent is absent, and
the free speech criterion and external observer pattern-recognition remain the only available
tools. The two diagnostics are therefore complementary rather than interchangeable: when the
effect is unobservable, intent may be detectable, and when intent is undetectable, the effect (or
its structural trace) may still be visible from outside. 
Together they cover more of the space of manipulation than either covers separately.

## 7 Scope

This account characterises manipulation as a mechanism. The question of when cost-imposition of
this kind is morally permissible is outside the scope of the present analysis. What is established
here is that manipulation-occurrence is independent of the moral status of the manipulation: 
a morally permissible manipulation
is still a manipulation, and the mechanism is the same regardless of whether it is sanctioned.
