# Pickle and Punishment

## Chapter one : The form of the argument

The Aldermast case had been dead for two weeks before Lena noticed it was still on her desk.

Not literally on her desk — she was not that kind of lawyer. It was in a folder in the third drawer, filed
under Closed, which was where it should have been. But she kept returning to it. She would be in the middle
of something else — a deposition, a lunch she was eating at her desk, a phone call she was half-listening
to — and she would pull the drawer open and find herself reading the same paragraph again. Section 4,
subsection 2(b). The part about proportionality.

The client had pushed her to plead down, but she had advised against it. The sentence was, in her view,
disproportionate to the offence: too heavy at the top of the range, too lenient at the bottom, the
guidelines themselves a kind of guesswork dressed in the language of precision. She had written a brief
making this case. The judge read it, set it down, and sentenced within guidelines. Lena had thanked the
court and returned to her office and put the case in the Closed drawer.

That was three months ago. The Aldermast folder had migrated from Closed to a stack on the left side of her
desk, then to the space beside her keyboard, then to a position directly in front of her monitor, forcing
her to play peek-a-boo every morning with her own screen.

She moved it again now, setting it on top of the filing cabinet rather than back in the drawer, which she
told herself was not the same as leaving it out.

* * *
She had been practising for five years. Her office was on the fourteenth floor of a glass building that
turned orange in the late afternoon. The view from her window showed a stretch of river, a bridge that was
always partially under construction, and on clear days the white antenna of a broadcast tower on a hill
twenty kilometres away.

Her specialty was civil procedure, which was the branch of law concerned not with whether the right outcome
had been reached but whether the right method had been followed to reach it. She had come to it not through
lack of interest in the outcomes — she was interested in outcomes, deeply — but through the conviction that
method was the only part of justice that could be made reliable. Outcomes depended on facts, and facts were
contested, and witnesses lied, and juries felt things.

Procedure was different. Procedure was a set of rules that either had been followed or had not. If you
built the procedure correctly, the outcomes would follow. This was, she knew, closer to philosophy, or
jurisprudence, than to practice — the kind of claim you made in a seminar, not in a courtroom. In a
courtroom you applied the rules.
She had presented a paper to this effect at a departmental seminar two years ago. It had gone well enough
that someone had suggested she write it up properly, which she thought about most nights but had not yet
done.

The Aldermast folder was visible from where she sat. The corner of it stuck out over the edge of the
cabinet.

* * *
The case that arrived on that Tuesday was not unusual. A family dispute, a restraining order violated, a
woman in the northern part of the city who had gone through three procedural appeals and was now asking for
a fourth. Lena read the file with her coffee going cold beside her and a pen moving in small circles in her
left hand, which was not the hand she wrote with.

The third appeal had failed on a technicality. A notification had not been sent within the required window
— forty-eight hours; it had been sent in fifty-one. Lena read this twice. She looked out the window at the
bridge. She looked back at the file.

The underlying ruling, if it stood, would require the woman to pay a sum she could not afford, for a
violation she had not committed, because a letter had been sent three hours late by someone in a municipal
office who had probably been handling six other cases at the same time.

This was, technically, correct. The rules had been applied. The procedure had been followed. A textbook
case, really. The kind they should put in the curriculum under "working as intended."

She put the file down. She picked up her coffee. It was cold. She put it down again.

There was a fourth appeal route — narrow, rarely used, requiring a demonstration of systemic procedural
failure rather than individual error. It was a long argument. She had made it twice before, successfully
once and not-successfully once, and both times it had taken the better part of six months.

She pulled a legal pad from the side drawer and began to write.

* * *
Her colleague Dav appeared in her doorway at half past five with his jacket over his arm, looking somewhat
hesitant, but clearly trying to act non-chalant.

"You coming to Riya and Pete’s later?," he said. 

It was a bit of a hard sell to get Lena to trade an evening with Tango for additional time spent with
colleagues. Tango, who could fall asleep in any position and frequently did, was at this moment almost
certainly upside down on the sofa with his ears inside out.

"Am I sensing a strong maybe?"

Lena looked at the legal pad. She had covered three pages without noticing. The handwriting at the bottom
of the third page was smaller than at the top, which happened when she had been writing fast. Some of the
writing was sideways.

"An hour," she said. "I'll come for an hour."

The dinner was about three wine glasses in. While all four colleagues were reasonably versed in both old-
and new-world offerings, they found themselves in agreement that wine could really benefit from labels
regularly commissioned from street artists. You can never accurately describe this Malbec, anyways, not
even with a picture, and especially not with words. And the artists are getting paid.

Shoptalk was an inevitability.

Riya asked about the Aldermast case and Lena somewhat ungracefully drifted towards the question of whether
the sentencing guidelines were a floor or a ceiling or merely a suggestion dressed up as architecture,
teetering on the precipice of entering “arguing with herself” territory. Lena was taken aback, or perhaps
intrigued, by her own arguments as if they were an interlocutor’s, punctuated by unrestrained
gesticulation.

On the walk home the city was cool and loud and the bridge was lit from below, orange against the black
water. She thought about systemic failure. She thought about the forty-eight-hour window and the three
hours that had swallowed a woman's case. She thought about the seminar at the university and what she had
said in it, and whether it still seemed true in the same way.

She had said: the procedure is the guarantee. If the procedure was followed, the outcome was legitimate.
That was what procedure was for.

She walked. The bridge slid into the fog behind her. Surely, the answer was simple - adjust the procedure
when it fails. We’ll get to the right procedure eventually. Oh, and the closer bridge to her house (and her
work) would reopen eventually.

* * *
The fourth appeal would take six months. She knew this, and began anyway, and worked on it in the hours
before her other work began and sometimes in the hours after it ended. The woman — her name was Brindt, she
had a daughter, she worked in a school — came to the office twice and left both times looking as though
something had been returned to her temporarily and might be taken back.

The building across the street was being renovated. Each morning Lena arrived to find it slightly
different: scaffolding moved, windows missing, then present again but different, the façade in a permanent
state of being made correct. She had stopped noticing it until she started noticing it again, around the
third month of the Brindt appeal, when she would sometimes stand at the window with her coffee and watch
the workers for a few minutes before sitting down.

The Aldermast folder was still on the filing cabinet. At some point she had moved it back to the desk.

* * *
The Brindt appeal failed that Tuesday. Not the fourth route — she had argued that well, and it had held —
but a parallel challenge the opposing counsel had filed six weeks in, which she had assessed as weak and
which the court had, to her considered surprise, agreed with and then built something unexpected from. The
ruling was forty pages. She read it in an afternoon, standing at her desk, not sitting down.

The ruling was not wrong. She could see the logic of it. If she had been the judge she would not have ruled
this way, but she could follow every step from premise to conclusion and find no place where the chain
broke. The procedure had been followed. The outcome was the outcome.

She called Brindt and delivered the news in the language she had been trained to use, which was precise and
not unkind and which said everything except what she was actually thinking. Brindt said thank you in a
voice like a door closing very quietly.

Lena put the phone down. She looked at the bridge. Some part of the undercarriage was wrapped in plastic
now, white and slightly inflated, like a bandage on something that wasn't healing.

She opened the Aldermast folder.

She read section 4, subsection 2(b) for what she calculated, without meaning to count, was the ninth time.

She had a case the next morning at nine. A straightforward thing, property dispute, client was reasonable,
the documents were in order. She would handle it the way she always did: methodically, without excess. She
was good at her work. She believed in her work.

She closed the folder.

She picked up her bag and her coat, then turned off the light. She stood for a moment in the lit rectangle
of the doorway, the office dark behind her, the corridor bright ahead.

## Chapter two : Bouncing

The invitation had come by text, which was most of Petra’s communication with Lena these days. They had
grown up together, going from hanging out at the mall most days, to, eventually, only the occasional text
and a tri-yearly group gettogether.

BBQ Sunday, ours, come any time after 2, bring nothing.

Lena brought nothing. Tango was at the cottage with her parents for the weekend. Lena had stayed behind
intending to catch up on work, and he had gone with the intention to splash around in the lake and
investigate whatever the ground smelled like.

She arrived at half past two, and stood at the edge of the garden for a moment before anyone noticed her.
She walked under an apple tree, past a bed of herbs by the shed, along a blooming vine wrapping around the
fence, and stopped next to two children's bicycles leaning against each other with the easy geometry of
things that belonged where they were. A folding table held plates and glasses and a cutting board with
something on it that Petra was still arranging when she looked up and waved.

"You came," Petra said, in a way that was warm and only slightly surprised.

"Well, I was going to loiter at the mall by myself..."

Petra hugged her briefly, the kind of hug that landed on one shoulder. "Come and meet — well, you know most
people."

Lena knew some of them. Petra's husband Ors, who worked in something municipal and asked careful questions.
A woman from Petra's work whose name Lena was given and immediately lost in the processing of the handshake
and the sun in her eyes. Two other couples she half-recognised from previous versions of afternoons like
this one. The children appeared and disappeared with the autonomy of small animals.

She found a chair near the herb bed and accepted a glass of something cold and tried not to sit too weird
or stare too long at anyone.

* * *
The food appeared in stages. Ors managed the grill with the focused pleasure of someone doing the one task
they had been allocated and doing it well. Petra moved between the kitchen and the table, adding things.

Petra was an adamant party planner. Somehow, she still believed in party favours even for adult BBQs with
no discernable theme. Pickling this summer’s cucumber bounty from the garden called to Petra - handmade
without being precious, themed without admitting to a theme. Hers was definitely a pickle family, too.

The jars were dark glass, labels handwritten in calligraphy, and with little ribbon bows. Inside, Lena
could see the ridged shapes of pickles in their brine, dense and greenish, packed close, with lots of
colourful herbs and spices.

Someone said something appreciative. Petra unscrewed the lid and the smell came out sharp and dill-y, and
the woman whose name Lena had lost said she hadn't had a proper pickle in years, and Ors called something
from the grill, and the afternoon continued.

Lena looked at the jar. It was the kind of afternoon that made old legislation feel very far away — the air
thick and still, the sort of heat that Connecticut had apparently once thought worth regulating pickles in.

* * *
It had started, as these things did, with someone saying something incorrect, jolting Lena away from
watching a bee work its way along the herb bed.

Ors's brother had mentioned a fine he'd received for parking in a “plates with first letters A to O only
zone” - never having encountered this being regulated before. This caused an expected and appropriately
mild uproar at the table. Inevitably, someone pointed out that it was funny how many completely absurd laws
were still technically in force, and that you're not allowed to sell a pickle in Connecticut unless it's
been certified, or something like that.


Lena set down her glass.

"The Connecticut pickle statute," she said. "1948. A pickle cannot legally be sold unless it bounces when
dropped from a height of one foot."

"The origin is actually a pair of cases," she continued, "Two dealers were selling pickles unfit for human
consumption”. The state agriculture commissioner — his name was Snowden — introduced the bounce test as a
quality standard. It became statute. It remains statute."

"That can't still be law," said the woman whose name she'd lost.

"It is still law," Lena said. She had said this sentence, about various laws, many times. It never lost its
particular satisfaction. "Completely unenforceable in any practical sense, but never repealed. There are
dozens like it. A law in Yorkshire once required that any Scotsman found within the city walls of York
after dark could be shot with a crossbow, provided it was not a Sunday. Still technically—"

"Should we test it?" said Lena.

She had not planned to say this. Or rather — she became aware, very slightly later than would have been
useful, that she had been intending to say it for approximately two minutes, had been culminating toward it
the way you build toward a conclusion that seems, from the inside, entirely inevitable.

She unceremoniously opened her pickler party packet (a jar) and picked up one of Petra's pickles.

She was already registering the shape of it, the weight, the satisfying absurdity of what she was about to
do, already savouring the wave of ecstasy she foresaw passing over the crowd (~10 middle aged adults eating
burgers) upon her bold and justice-affirming actions, she dropped it onto the paving stone.

It did not bounce. It split, wetly, and the brine went across the stone and across Petra's shoe, and a
piece of it came to rest against the leg of the folding table.

The garden was quiet in the way that gardens go quiet when something has happened in them.

Lena looked at the piece of pickle against the table leg. She looked at Petra's shoe. She looked at Petra's
face, which was doing something she needed a moment to read, and by the time she had read it the moment had
already passed through several of its stages without her.

"I'll—" Lena started.

"It's fine," Petra said, and went inside.

* * *
Ors brought out the meat and the afternoon resumed, the way afternoons do when the people in them have
decided to let them resume. Someone poured more wine, pickles became a taboo topic, and lawn fertilizer got
its moment in the conversational sun. The children appeared and ate standing up and disappeared again.

Lena ate and answered questions when questions were directed at her and watched Petra from across the
table. Petra talked to the people beside her. She laughed once, at something Ors said. She reached across
him to pass the salt to someone, the gesture easy and automatic, the whole afternoon still working in her
hands.

She did not look at Lena.

This was not unusual. Petra often didn't look at her much at these things, busy hosting. Lena knew this.
She held her glass and reminded herself of this with a thoroughness that was its own kind of signal, if she
had been disposed to read it.

* * *
She left at six, which felt right. She said goodbye to Ors, who shook her hand with the genuine warmth of a
man who finds most people easy. She said goodbye to the other guests, the ones still there. She found Petra
in the kitchen, covering things with cling film.

"Thank you for having me," Lena said.

Petra looked up. There was a version of this look that Lena had been receiving since they were children —
since school, the mall hangouts, since the family gatherings where Lena had explained things to adults at
length. The look said several things at once and the things were not all the same kind of thing.

"I'm sorry about breaking your pickle," Lena said. 

Petra set down the cling film. "Lena," she said, and then didn't say anything else for a moment, and in
that moment the kitchen held the sound of the garden outside and someone's child somewhere asking a
question that went unanswered.

"It wasn't about the pickle," Petra said.

Lena waited for the rest of this sentence. It didn't come.

"Okay," Lena said.

She walked home. The evening was cooling and the streets were still carrying the day's heat from the paving
and somewhere nearby someone was finishing their own barbecue, the smoke reaching her a block before she
expected it. She thought about the bounce test. She thought about the two dealers in 1948 and the
agriculture commissioner named Snowden and the standard he had introduced to protect people from harm and
how it had become, over seventy-odd years, a fact so inert it could be dropped at a dinner table as a joke.

She thought about Petra's face. She thought about the effect she had on the party. It wasn’t a wave of
ecstasy. The gift of the faulty (by Connecticut standards) pickles was received much better than her own
gift of the pickle revelation.

The street was quiet. A cat sat on a wall and watched her pass with the total indifference of something
that had been there longer than she had.

She let herself into her flat and stood in the hallway for a moment with her keys still in her hand. The
lack of tail-wagging was almost giving her tinnitus. Then she put the keys on the hook, and went to the
kitchen, and stood at the counter, and did not make tea. She did, however, snack on one of the remaining
pickles. It was crunchy and delicious.


## Chapter three : The Pickle of Consequences

The text arrived on Tuesday morning, two days after the BBQ, while Lena was reading a motion she had
already read twice. She noticed the notification and set it aside the way she set aside anything that
wasn't the motion, and then picked up her phone four minutes later when the motion stopped cooperating.

Petra, 9:14am
Hey. Sorry about Sunday, I think I was a bit off with you. It's been a weird summer.

Lena read this and put the phone face-down and read another paragraph of the motion. Then she picked the
phone up again.

Lena, 9:22am
No, I'm sorry. I should have thought before I did that. 

Petra, 9:24am
Well, it was just a pickle.
Lena looked at this. It wasn't about the pickle, Petra had said in the kitchen. Now it was just a pickle.
She typed, “I know, but I really made it weird with it”, and deleted it, and typed “I know, but is there
some way i can make it up to you?”, deleted it, and sent nothing.

Petra texted again before she'd decided what to say.

Petra, 9:31am
Can I ask you something. Do you ever think about how things land? Like when you do something. Whether you
think about how it's going to land before you do it.

The motion was still open on her desk. Through the window the building across the street had acquired a new
section of scaffolding overnight, steel and orange, precise against the grey of the upper floors.

Lena, 9:38am
Yes. I thought it would be funny.

Petra, 9:39am
I know you did.

Then, after a pause long enough that Lena had put the phone down and returned to the motion:

Petra, 9:47am
I just think there should be consequences sometimes. Not punishment. Just — consequences. 
Does that make sense.

Lena read this three times. She set the phone down very deliberately, the way you set something down when
you want to be the kind of person who sets things down deliberately.

“Consequences?! I’m a lawyer! And what are these specific ones really for, huh? Being awkward? Unaware of
unspoken vague social rules? Obsessed with the minutia of the law? Making someone feel bad for pickling
wrong?”

Even now, even in the midst of this outburst, it was obvious to Lena that she had indeed done something
wrong, but she still wished she could take back her apology.

Lena picked up the motion. She read the same paragraph again. The words were in the right order and she
could not tell what they meant.

* * *
The letter arrived on Thursday. Not to her office — to her home address, which meant it had come from a
municipal registry, not a court filing system. She found it between a utilities bill and something from her
building's management company, the envelope unremarkable, her name in a font she associated with form
letters and appointment reminders.

Getting home from work, she opened it in the hallway, still in her coat. Tango attempted his best
greetings, including a full-body wiggle, a heavy lean, and the downward dog. The letter did, as expected,
outline some very clear rules, and they were indeed tangentially related to pickles:


Notice of infraction — Municipal Code Division / Dignitary Offences
It has been determined that on [date] you did, at a private gathering at [address], wilfully disrespect
food prepared and offered in good faith by a resident of this municipality, in contravention of Section
14(c) of the Domestic Dignitary Provisions Act (1931), as amended. You are required to attend an assessment
appointment at the address below within fourteen days of this notice. Failure to attend will result in
escalation of proceedings.


She read it twice. Then she read it a third time more slowly, the way she read things she needed to be
certain she had read correctly.

Section 14(c). She knew the Domestic Dignitary Provisions Act (DDPA). She had cited it once, obliquely, in
a footnote about the historical treatment of household offences in a paper she had written during her
second year of practice. She had not cited it because it was relevant. She had cited it because it was old
and because the footnote needed one more source and because the Act had struck her, at the time, as a
curiosity — a fossil of a legal era in which the domestic sphere had its own parallel apparatus of minor
sanctions, most of which had been superseded, consolidated, or quietly forgotten.

Section 14(c) had not, apparently, been superseded, consolidated, or quietly forgotten.

Noticing that she was still standing in the hallway in her coat, she hung it up, went to the kitchen, and
made tea. She actually made it this time, and stood at the counter while the kettle boiled and looked at
the letter again.

“Wilfully disrespect food prepared and offered in good faith.”

The tea was too hot to drink. She held it anyway, hands wrapping around the scorching mug itself. Her
family was not usually litigious…


* * *
She looked up the assessment process that evening, professionally and thoroughly, the way she looked up
anything she needed to understand. The Domestic Dignitary Provisions Act had seventeen sections. Section 14
covered offences relating to the household — to guests, to property, to the specific category of food and
domestic labour offered in a spirit of hospitality. The Act had been amended four times since 1931, most
recently in 2009, when the assessment and rehabilitation framework had been attached to it as part of a
broader reform package she vaguely remembered reading about and then not thinking about again.

The reform package had been, in her recollection, considered progressive. The fourth amendment, added in
2009 as a pilot programme, was where the language changed. The preceding sections were drafted in the
manner she expected — dense, specific, cross-referenced.

Section 4(d) was different. It described a confidentiality provision attached to the restorative processing
agreement, the contents of which were to be disclosed only to the offender and their assigned processing
officer. The confidentiality provision was described in unusually vague language, which is its own kind of
bureaucratic mystery. No expiry date, no review mechanism, just sitting there in the amendment. Not their
legal representative. Not the court. The stated purpose was the protection of the offender's privacy during
rehabilitation.

She read it again. The provision was described in terms of its existence and its scope — who could know
about it, when it was triggered — but not its contents. The contents, the legislation said, were at the
discretion of the processing officer in accordance with departmental guidance.

Departmental guidance was not legislation. It was not publicly available in the index she was using.

Surely, there was some interesting precedent here. She made a note, circled it, and moved on. 

She dove into the details this time around, making notes on a legal pad in handwriting that started at a
normal size and got smaller as the evening went on.

At eleven she closed her laptop and looked at the notes. The restorative processing framework was, as far
as she could determine, mandatory for first offences under Section 14. There was an assessment, and then
there was a programme, and then there was the confidentiality provision she would need to sign, the
description of which read unnecessarily vague.

Lena was deep into lawyer mode. She had a background in formal logic, having spent two years in grad
school, during which she had also, somehow, raised Tango. Treating family, BBQs, and pickles as just items
to which one must correctly apply the axioms of the law was, at least, a mode she knew how to inhabit.

However, it had occurred to her, early in her practice — too early, before she had the vocabulary to
articulate it — that law and formal logic were not the same thing dressed differently. They looked the same
from the outside: premises, rules, conclusions. The satisfaction of a well-formed argument. But formal
logic was self-contained by design. Its symbols didn't have to mean anything outside the system. Law did.
Law had to touch the world, had to be applied to situations that hadn't been anticipated when the rules
were written, had to produce outcomes for people who were not symbols.

She had found this genuinely upsetting at the time. She had gone into law because it was the closest thing
available to formal logic that also mattered. The discovery that it was imprecise by design had taken her
several years to fully absorb, and she was not certain she had finished absorbing it.

Nevertheless, she knew the procedural routes and the arguments and the likelihood of success for each of
them. She ran through these now, as she would for a client, and arrived at the same place she usually
arrived when she ran through procedural routes honestly: the most technically correct path and the most
practically sensible path were not the same path, and the gap between them was where most of the misery
lived.

She thought about Brindt, saying thank you in a voice like a door closing. 

She put the legal pad in her bag for the morning and went to bed and lay in the dark for a while, listening
to Tango snore, thinking about the word wilfully.


## Chapter four : Guilty

She had told Dav on a Wednesday, in the corridor outside the kitchen, in the thirty seconds between him
finishing a call and her having to be somewhere else. She had not planned to tell him then. She had been
telling herself she would tell him at a reasonable moment, and had been finding reasons why each moment was
not yet reasonable, and then the corridor happened and she said it before the calculation completed. His
initial reaction was reserved and professional, but right before she continued on down the corridor,

“You really chose the only profession for which your faux pas seems as appropriate as it would for a
rambunctious 10-year old, but for entirely different reasons”

A few days later they had once more encountered each other by the kitchen. Dav dared to bring it up,

"So, The pickle thing."

"The pickle thing."

He had looked at her for another moment. He genuinely did not know if they had the kind of relationship
where offering to come to another’s court proceedings was appropriate. She did not know this either. Is
this … getting irreversibly involved in each others’ lives, or just some extracurricular bonding time
between colleagues?

"Do you want me to come?"

She had said yes before she decided to.

* * *
He picked her up at eight fifteen. His car felt somewhat familiar from previous work-related carpools. 

"It won't touch your licence," he said, at the first set of lights. "Section 14 is a dignitary offence.
It's not professional misconduct. It won't even show up in the standard disclosure check."

"I know," she said.

"I'm just saying."

"I know. Thank you."

The lights changed. He drove. The river was visible for a stretch, grey and flat in the morning light, and
she watched it until the road turned away from it.

"A pickle," he said, after a while. Not unkindly. Just — still processing.

"A pickle," she confirmed.

"And the law in question was from—"

"1948. Connecticut."

"Right." A pause. "I have to say, professionally speaking, I am extremely curious about what happens in
there."

"Professionally speaking," she said.

"Entirely professionally."

She looked at the pine tree swaying slightly as he took a corner. "Me too," she said.

* * *
The courthouse was the one she used for procedural hearings — she had been in this building perhaps forty
times. She knew where the lifts were, knew which clerk was efficient and which one needed a second ask,
knew the particular acoustic quality of the third floor corridor where sound arrived slightly before you
expected it. She had never been on this side of the building before. The rooms for minor infractions were
on the ground floor, at the end of a corridor she had walked past without registering.

There were four people ahead of her. She sat in a row of chairs bolted to the wall, put her bag on her lap,
and looked at the floor. Dav sat beside her and looked at the floor too, in the companionable way of
someone who understands that looking at the floor is currently the appropriate activity.

The four people ahead of her took, collectively, less time than she had expected. The woman at the front of
the queue was in and out in six minutes. The man after her in four. She had prepared herself for a wait —
had told herself it could be hours, had seen clients sit in corridors like this for most of a morning — and
instead the queue moved steadily forward with the brisk indifference of a system that had processed this
particular category of thing many times and had got quite efficient at it.

Her name was called at nine forty-three.

* * *
The room was smaller than a courtroom, larger than an office. A magistrate sat behind a raised desk — not a
judge, a magistrate, which was procedurally correct for a Section 14 infraction and which she noted with
the automatic reflex of someone who catalogues these things. A clerk sat to the side. There was no public
gallery. There was no opposing counsel. There was a chair for her, and a microphone she did not need to use
because the room was not large enough to require one, and a document on the desk that she could see from
where she stood contained her name and the date and an address she recognised as Petra's.

The magistrate read the charge.

She had heard charges read aloud many times. She had stood beside clients while it happened and had
monitored their reactions and had sometimes put a hand briefly on an arm to steady something. She had not
anticipated that hearing her own name in that particular grammatical position — the party named above did
wilfully — would feel the way it felt, which was less bad than she had expected and more strange.

"How do you plead?"

"Guilty," she said.

The magistrate made a note. The clerk made a note. The room held the small silence of a transaction
completing.

"You are hereby referred to the Restorative Processing Unit for assessment and programme allocation under
Section 14(c) of the Domestic Dignitary Provisions Act, as amended. You will receive written confirmation
of your assessment appointment within five working days."

She had known this was coming. She had read the legislation, followed the mandatory referral pathway, and
had known since the evening she looked it up that this was the only route. Knowing had not entirely
prepared her for the magistrate saying it aloud in a room with her name on a document, in a building she
had been in forty times, at the end of a corridor she had never noticed.

"Thank you" 

* * *
Dav was where she had left him, in the bolted chair, looking at his phone. He stood when he saw her,
searching her face for information.

"Done," she said.

"That was fast."

"Yes."

They walked back down the corridor she had never noticed and through the building she knew well and out
into the morning, which was going about its business with complete indifference to what had just happened
on the ground floor. Dav unlocked the car and they got in. The pine tree swayed.

"So," he said, pulling out. "The Restorative Processing Unit."

"Apparently."

"I've never heard of it."

"Neither had I," she said. "Not really."

He drove. The river reappeared on the left, the same grey and flat as before, but with an entirely
different landscape of buildings reflecting in it.

"How do you feel?" he said.

She thought about this for a moment, genuinely, the way she thought about questions she hadn't
pre-answered. "Like something happened that I don't fully have the vocabulary for yet," she said. I think
I’ll know better after I talk to Petra and figure this Restorative Processing Unit thing out.

Dav nodded slowly. "That tracks," he said.

They drove the rest of the way without talking, trying to compose themselves before switching to working on
their own cases.

## Chapter five : The assessment

Walking along the river, Lena was contemplating her choices. Her guilty plea – it was part morbid
curiosity, part desire to get first-hand experience with the other side of what she does, what her clients
have to go through. Another part was the vague feeling that for the sake of her own family situation, a
guilty plea and an apology had seemed like a reasonable price to pay. She was approaching her destination,
and wondering if she was about to get her morbid curiosity satisfied.

The office was on the third floor of a building she had walked past many times without registering. It sat
between a pharmacy and a dry cleaner on a street she used as a shortcut. A small brass plate by the door,

Restorative Processing Unit. Third floor.

She had not taken a lawyer. She had considered it and decided against — partly because she felt
uncomfortable withholding information from her representation as per the confidentiality agreement. And,
partly because arriving with counsel to an assessment for dropping a pickle felt like a statement she
wasn't prepared to make. She had armed herself with a fresh legal pad and a fancy new pen.

After eleven minutes of waiting, a door opened and a woman appeared in it and said her name.

* * *
According to her nameplate, the woman's name was Maren. No last name. She was perhaps forty, perhaps older,
with the kind of face that gave Lena very little to work with.

The office was small and tidy. A shelf held binders with labels Lena could not read from where she sat. A
plant on the windowsill was either very healthy or artificial — she found herself oddly distracted in her
attempts to resolve this question.

"Thank you for coming in," Maren said. She had a file open in front of her, which she did not look at.
"I'll explain how this works and then we'll go through the documentation together. You're welcome to ask
questions as we go."

"Sounds good," Lena said.

"The assessment is the first stage of the restorative processing framework. Its purpose is to establish the
facts of the infraction, confirm your suitability for the programme, and give you the information you need
to make a decision about how to proceed."

In a clear and measured tone, she continued, "The programme itself has three components: acknowledgement,
which is a structured process of understanding the impact of the infraction; restitution, which in cases
under Section 14 typically takes the form of a documented act of repair toward the complainant; and the
confidentiality instrument, which I'll explain separately."

"The confidentiality instrument," Lena said. "That's the provision in Section 4(d)."

Maren looked at her for a moment. "You've read the Act."

"I've read the Act."

"Then you'll know the instrument is part of the pilot programme for first offences under Section 14." She
reached into the file and produced a document, several pages, and set it on the desk between them. "This is
Form 7. The instrument is attached as Schedule B."

Lena picked it up.

* * *
Form 7 itself was standard — her name, the infraction, the programme components, a signature block.
Schedule B was four pages. The language in the first three was what she had expected from the Act: scope of
confidentiality, parties covered, duration. The fourth page was where the language changed.

Form 7 — Schedule B / Confidentiality Instrument, Section 4
The party agrees that the specific nature and administration of measures applied under this programme shall
not be disclosed to any third party, including but not limited to the complainant, in any form that would
allow a reasonable person to infer the character of those measures. The party further agrees that, for the
purposes of any communication with the complainant or their representatives, the measures shall be
described, if described at all, only in terms consistent with standard programme outcomes as advised by the
processing officer. Non-compliance shall constitute breach and shall result in: (i) reinstatement of
original proceedings in full, as detailed in Schedule C; and (ii) mandatory service obligations as
specified in Appendix 4(ii) of the Departmental Labour Provisions.

She flipped to Appendix 4(ii). The page was not there. Schedule B ended at page four. She went back through
the document, methodically, in case she had missed a page. She had not missed a page.

"Appendix 4(ii)?" 

"It's a departmental document. It isn't included in the public-facing materials."

"I can't see what I'm agreeing to."

"You're agreeing to the service obligations as specified. The specifics are determined if and when a breach
occurs."

Lena looked at the empty space where the appendix should have been. She looked up at Maren.

She read it again.

“Consistent with standard programme outcomes as advised by the processing officer.”

She set the document down and looked at Maren, who was waiting with the particular patience of someone who
had learned that this was the part that took time.

“Communications with the complainant had to be consistent with standard outcomes. The complainant would be
informed the sentence had been administered.”

The fog of bureaucratic jargon began to dissipate, revealing meaning that was no less confusing than the
jargon itself.

"So… the complainant will think I was punished," Lena said. "In a way that I wasn't."

Maren said nothing.

"The programme is restorative," Lena said, almost to herself. "The outcomes are real." She was not asking.

"Yes," Maren said. “Your case has been determined to be a good candidate for this pilot programme”.

They looked at each other across the desk. She had sat across from people in rooms like this, never having
considered what the room looked like from this side of the desk.

* * *
"If I breach the instrument," Lena said. "If I tell someone — the complainant, anyone — what the programme
actually involves. How does that get enforced?"

"Original proceedings are reinstated."

"Yes, I read that. I'm asking how you would know. If I told someone. How the breach would be detected."

Maren considered this for a moment that was slightly longer than a routine pause. "The instrument is a
legal agreement. Breach has consequences."

"That's not an answer either."

Lena looked at the plant on the windowsill. She had still not determined whether it was real. This question
seemed more important than ever. She asked Maren to touch her plant, then quickly apologized for “crossing
a line”, as she could not find a better way to summarize the nature of her social transgression.

"Most people," Maren said, "find that the question of enforcement becomes less pressing once they've
completed the programme."

"Why?"

Maren looked at her with something that was not quite sympathy and not quite its absence. "Because by then,
it has served its purpose."

* * *
Lena was given three days for consideration. There was a number to call in case she had further questions,
but based on today’s experience, the answers would not be satisfactory.

Lena took the documents and put them in her bag. She stood, Maren stood, and they shook hands across the
desk with the formality of two people who have conducted a … transaction?

In the lift she looked at the floor numbers descending quickly, feeling stationary in a moving box. She
thought about how in old videogames, sometimes the ground moves at the wrong speed under a character’s
feet.

She came out onto the street. The pharmacy was open, its lights very white. The dry cleaner had a sign in
the window she had never noticed before. She stood on the pavement for a moment and looked at the brass
plate by the door she had just come through. She had been walking past it for three years.

## Chapter six : Three days

The documents sat on her kitchen table when she got home. She put them there deliberately, in the centre,
so she would have to look at them. Then she fed Tango and stood at the counter while he ate and looked at
the documents from across the room.

Tango finished eating and came to stand beside her, the ridge of fur along his back catching the kitchen
light. He had the specific patience of a dog who has spent nearly a decade learning the rhythms of one
person. He did not ask questions, nor did he spill any state-mandated secrets. Plus, all that formal logic
experience.

"It's not going to affect my life in any significant way," she told him. "That's the thing. The programme
is what, three months. The restitution is a documented act of repair — I issue a formal apology, I bring a
food offering – of all things, it's all on record. Then it's done. My licence is unaffected. My cases are
unaffected." She paused. "Petra gets told something about my sentence that may not be accurate, and I hold
my tongue."

Tango's back fur ridge shifted slightly as he turned to look at the door, then back at her.

"I know," she said.

* * *
She went to work the next morning and handled a property dispute with the methodical efficiency it
required, then ate lunch at her desk and read a filing that had come in from opposing counsel on a contract
case. The filing was competent. The argument was sound. She annotated it in the margins the way she always
did, small precise marks, and at some point became aware that she had written the word wilfully in a margin
where it did not belong and crossed it out.

At three o'clock Dav appeared in her doorway with two coffees and the expression that meant he had noticed
something and had decided to approach it indirectly.

"You seem like you're somewhere else," he said, setting a coffee on her desk.

"I'm here," she said.

"You're physically here," he said. "The rest of you seems to be working something out."

She looked at the coffee. She thought about the confidentiality instrument, the clause covering third
parties, the definition of disclosure. She thought about how she would characterise what she was working
out in terms that were technically accurate and entirely uninformative.

"Just a personal thing," she said. "It'll resolve."

The building across the street had lost another section of scaffolding. The renovation was nearly done. The
façade was clean and uniform now, every window in place, the surface smooth and unbroken. It looked, she
thought, exactly like a building that had never needed any work.

* * *
That evening she took Tango to the park, let him off the leash, and walked the perimeter while he conducted
his investigations at his own pace.

She watched a man on a bench reading something on his phone. What procedures were going to be governing his
day tomorrow? Who is he mandated to give food respectfully to tomorrow? Did he absent-mindedly sign on to
the human centipede terms of service (a Southpark reference), or were they somehow state-mandated for him
also? What minor statutes of the municipal code were laying in wait for his own pickle slip-up? She had
known about these her whole career, and often found them interesting. They had seemed, until recently, to
belong to a different category than the work she actually did — curiosities, footnotes, the sediment of
legal history that nobody bothered to dredge.

Tango reappeared at her side, ridge up, carrying a stick he had found and immediately lost interest in. He
was walking under a sign in the park saying dogs are only allowed off-leash 7:30-11 pm. She clipped the
lead back on and they walked home.

* * *
On the second evening she looked up the cases she had handled in the past year and read through them with
the documents from Maren's office open beside her on the table. Unsure what she was looking for, she found
some minor procedural corrections that needed to be done, then moved on.

“I wonder how many of the people on the other side of those cases had sat at a kitchen table with a
document they didn't fully understand, working out what it would cost them to sign it?”

Tango put his head on her knee. She put her hand on his back, tugging slightly at his ridge.

"The thing is," she said, "the restitution is real, the apology is real, the food i have to make is real”.
Those are real things that actually happen between Petra and I. The victim”,  she paused before and after
this word, “cannot be lied to about these.”

She stared at Tango intensely. "The lie — if it's a lie — is only whatever Petra is told happens to me as
punishment. And Petra—" She stopped.

She had not let herself think directly about Petra since the assessment. She did so now, carefully, the way
you press on something to see if it still hurts. They haven’t talked since, but it had definitely crossed
her mind that something must really be wrong in Petra’s own life to file this unhinged lawsuit.

Petra had filed a complaint. Petra had also texted the next morning to apologise. Petra had said “I just
think there should be consequences sometimes”, and by the time the letter arrived had probably already
half-regretted it and half-not, and was now somewhere on the other side of the city in her worked-at
garden, not knowing what would happen next, waiting to be told something.

Whatever she was told, she would believe it. Petra has no reason to doubt the legal system in this
particular case, or her cousin. Petra would believe it. Either that would be enough for Petra or it
wouldn't, and either way it would have nothing to do with what had actually happened in Maren's office.

Tango shifted, pulling the fur ridge out of her grasp.

"I know," she said again.

* * *
On the third morning she woke early and made coffee and sat at the kitchen table with the documents in
front of her and a pen beside them. She had not put the pen there the night before. She was not certain
when she had put it there.

Tango lay under the table, pinning her feet to the ground. 

She read Schedule B one more time. She read the clause about standard programme outcomes. She read the part
about Appendix 4(ii) and the empty space where it wasn't. She read the signature block, which was simple —
her name, the date, a line.

She thought about Brindt. She thought about the letter sent three hours too late, and the woman who paid
for it. While being a mere failing of procedure, this felt much more like real punishment than her weird
contractual lie obligation. She thought about her paper on procedural jurisprudence, the one she still
hadn't written up, and what she would say in it now that she hadn't said in the seminar.

She thought about the building across the street, the façade smooth and complete, looking exactly like a
building that had never needed any work.

She picked up the pen. As she signed, she focused on the shape and placement of her signature, almost
ignoring the weight of the decision. “June is the sixth month… of what year?”

Tango's weight shifted against her feet.

Outside, the street was beginning its ordinary morning. As usual, traffic was all going the correct
direction, even though “drive on right” is just a social construct.

Clutching a coffee like it was going to get her through the morning, she headed to work. 


## Chapter seven : Standard programme outcomes

The acknowledgement sessions were held in a different room from Maren's office — smaller, with two chairs
at an angle rather than a desk between them, which Lena recognised as a deliberate architectural choice
about power dynamics. She noted this and sat down anyway.

There were four sessions over six weeks. The first was mostly Maren asking questions and Lena answering
them with the careful precision of someone who has spent five years learning that the way you say something
is as consequential as what you say. Lena has had a lot of practice keeping her cool, even steeped in a bit
of resentment. She mused that due to the nature of the proceedings, many people became agitated and
confused regardless, so Maren’s learned lesson may have actually been imagined to make her job feel more
like a personal journey.

Maren asked the same questions slightly differently until the answers started coming out at different
angles, and Lena became aware, somewhere in the third session, that she had been answering honestly for
some time without deciding to. Having four sessions seemed like overkill for producing an outcome with the
“least possible effort”, but this was a pilot project after all, and the data was very valuable for its
assessment.

The questions were not what she had expected. Not: what did you do. Not: do you understand that it was
wrong. More like: what was happening for you in the weeks before. What does Petra mean to you. When did you
last feel that an afternoon had gone the way you hoped. How do you feel about Petra not knowing?

She thought about Brindt. She thought about the Aldermast folder migrating across her desk for three
months, the corner of it visible from wherever she sat. She thought about the dinner at Riya's, and the
takeover of the conversation with the argument she'd been having with herself – the one about whether the
procedure was the guarantee or whether the guarantee was something the procedure was supposed to produce
and kept failing to. She thought about the contrast of the pickle flavour with its failure to abide by
pickle regulations.

She did not say most of this to Maren, but suspected she somehow got the gist.

"You work in procedure," Maren said, in the third session.

"Civil procedure. Yes."

"And you believe in it."

Lena considered this. "I believe it's the most reliable part of the system," she said. "I believe in it the
way you believe in the best available option."

Maren wrote something. Lena had given up trying to read what she wrote.

* * *
The restitution took longer to arrange than she had expected, partly because the documented act of repair
had to be agreed between her, Maren, and the complainant's processing liaison, and partly because the first
two things she proposed were declined as insufficient and she had to sit with that for a week before coming
back with something that was accepted.

What was accepted was this: a written acknowledgement of the specific harm caused, hand-delivered; a jar of
pickles made to Petra's own recipe, which Lena had to obtain from Petra's mother through a chain of family
communication she found mildly excruciating; and a meal, cooked by Lena, at a time of Petra's choosing.

The pickle jar was the part she thought about most. Obtaining the recipe had required asking her mom, who
had called Petra's mother, who had sent it via a photograph of a handwritten card that was slightly out of
focus. She had made three test batches before she was satisfied, and Tango had supervised all three from
his position under the kitchen table, occasionally raising his head when the vinegar smell became
pronounced.

She had gone into law because it was the closest thing to formal logic that also mattered. She had spent a
weekend making pickles, which, somehow, was the most logically and legally congruent part of this whole
saga. Tangible, real, no-nonsense pickles.

* * *
The delivery was on a Saturday morning in October. Petra answered the door in the particular way of someone
who had been expecting a knock and had decided in advance how to receive it — composed, slightly formal,
the children audible somewhere behind her.

Lena handed over the jar and the letter. Petra looked at both. The label on the jar was handwritten, Lena's
own handwriting, which was small and precise and looked nothing like Petra's original.

"So, you made them?"

"Three batches," Lena said. "The first two were wrong."

"Come on in."

* * *
They had coffee in the kitchen, which was the same kitchen and also slightly different — a child's drawing
had appeared on the fridge, a basket of apples that were, perhaps, from that tree. Ors was somewhere in the
house, present but not appearing, which felt like a courtesy.

Petra read the letter while Lena held her coffee. It was not a long letter. Lena had written seven drafts
and the final version was the shortest, which was usually how it went with the things that mattered.

"This is good," Petra said, setting it down. Not effusively. Just: this is good. The way you'd say it about
a piece of work that had been done properly. Lena smiled.

Petra looked at her for a moment. Lena knew what conversation was coming.

"They told me," Petra said. "About the community service."

Lena held her coffee cup. "Yes," she said.

"I wasn't — I didn't want this to be too rough for you." Petra turned the letter over in her hands. "I just
wanted there to be something. Some — weight to it. In the heat of the moment, I really wanted you to feel
as bad as I did, and not just because of the pickle. It brought back all the other times you… ” She paused.

"I know," Lena said.

"Was it—" Petra stopped. Started again. "Was it awful?"

Lena looked at the juicy apples. "It was fine, but I'd rather not talk about it," she said.

Petra nodded, slowly, in a way that meant she had understood something — not necessarily the right thing,
but something that satisfied her. "Okay," she said. "Okay."

They finished their coffees. Petra asked about Tango. Lena asked about the children. The kitchen held them
in its ordinary way, a gust of the autumn breeze wafting the rosemary smell into the window.

At the door, Petra hugged her — properly this time, both shoulders.

* * *
That evening, Petra stood at the kitchen counter while Ors made dinner. She told him about it, which gave
her even more relief than just talking to Lena.

"She made the pickles herself," Petra said. "Three batches."

Ors turned from the stove. "Really."

"And the letter was — it was right. It said the right things. Not like a legal document." She paused.
"Actually not at all like a legal document."

"She's a lawyer," Ors said, carefully.

"I know. That's what I mean." Petra picked up a piece of pepper from the cutting board and ate it. "She did
law too, you know. Same university. She was deciding between tax and contract law, I think, before she
changed her mind. Not sure why – formalizing tax law would have been right up her alley."

"Is that why you filed? Trying to speak to her in a language she’d understand?"

Petra was quiet for a moment. "I knew she'd understand the process, and even, for once, what I was actually
thinking" she said. "I knew it wouldn't be — I knew she could handle it."

Ors turned back to the stove. The oil was hot. He added the onions and the kitchen filled with the sound of
it.

"There's something slightly odd about it," Petra said. "The whole thing. I can't put my finger on it."

"Odd how?"

"I don't know. It all felt very — administered. Like it was designed to feel like the right amount." She
picked up another piece of pepper. "Which I suppose it was."

"And does it?" Ors said. "Feel like the right amount?"

Petra thought about this for a moment, genuinely, the way she thought about things she hadn't pre-answered.

"Yes," she said. "Mostly." She looked at the apples. "She said she'd rather not talk about the community
service."

"That sounds like Lena."

"It does," Petra said. "That's what's strange. It all sounds exactly like Lena, she seems fine with it, and
it all felt exactly like the right amount. I still can't decide if those two things are related or not."

Ors plated the onions. The kitchen was warm. Outside, the garden was dark, but the few flowers still
remaining on the apple tree flickered a little pink in kitchen light spilling through the window.

"You were at university together, eh?" Ors said. "You and Lena."

"First year," Petra said. "Before she transferred to law. We were closer then. I think I let a lot of stuff
slide because we used to talk about everything — justice, how the world should work, whether rules existed
to serve people or the other way around. She was the most interesting person I'd met. But even now, she
brings a lot of chaos to my barbecues." She paused. "She hasn't changed. That's the thing. She never
changes."

"It’s really hard to change an established dynamic.”

"I filed it before I could think about it too hard," Petra said. "Which is maybe the point."

"The point being?"

"That if I'd thought about it I wouldn't have done it. And I probably should have done it years ago. But,
you know, I actually have been saying it for like, fifteen years. Just not in a language she could
process."

Ors moved the onions around the pan. "Do you think she actually … learned something, though? From all of
this?"

Petra opened her mouth and then didn't say anything for a moment.

"She already knew," she said, slowly. "That's the thing. She knew her actions hurt people. She knew she'd
have to apologise for things she didn't realise at the time were wrong. She wrote it in the letter — she
said exactly that, actually, and it was right." She paused. "But she still can't tell in advance. She
couldn't then and she can't now. That's just — that's just who she is."

"So what did she learn?"

Petra looked at the letter on the counter. "Nothing she didn't already know," she said. "Nothing that's
going to change anything, really."

Ors turned the heat down. "So, it was mostly just for you then?"

The kitchen was quiet for a moment except for the low sound of the oil.

"Me," Petra said. "I think it was just for me." She said it without much drama, the way you say something
you've just realised is true and aren't sure yet how you feel about it. "Which is — I don't know. Is that
okay?"

"You’ll have to talk about this with her some more, it could bring you guys closer again."

Petra picked up the letter and looked at it again. “Like, we’re all gonna still make mistakes in the
future, but this whole ordeal makes me feel like im not always the one on the receiving side, left picking
up proverbial roadside trash at my own party”.

She put the letter on the fridge, under the child's drawing, and went to set the table.

## Chapter eight : Community service

The Saturday of her first scheduled community service she woke at seven, made coffee, and stood at the
kitchen window for a while watching the street. Tango stood beside her and watched it too, with the focused
attention of a dog who has decided that whatever she is looking at must be worth looking at.

She was not picking up roadside trash. She was going to do the dishes.

This felt, in a way she didn't examine too closely, appropriate.

* * *
The dishes took about twenty minutes. Tango supervised from the doorway. She washed everything including
the things that could have gone in the dishwasher, which she only did when she needed her hands occupied
and her mind elsewhere.

That elsewhere was this: punishment, as she had always understood it, was a transaction. It was an entirely
separate thing from restitution or rehabilitation. Rehabilitation is for the perpetrator – they’re a better
person for it because they understand they hurt someone and seem like they won’t do it again. Restitution
is for the victim - their life is, theoretically, restored to its previous level according to some
calculus. Usually, this is by way of the offender paying the victim money, issuing an apology, or… making
them food? She made a mental note to cite some of her favourite works here, especially Zehr’s.

Then, mid-dishes, she looked up, shook her hands dry, splashing Tango in the eye, and made a semi-wet
physical note on a nearby scrap of paper. Between her palms, she was finally forming the golf-ball-sized
core of paper she had been planning for years, crafted out of the snow of legal expertise and an inherently
strong sense of justice.

“But where does punishment fit in?”. Many a literary and philosophical giant has thought about this before
her. And clearly, also some oddball with a lot of legislative influence in Connecticut in the late 2000s.
She would for sure get down to the bottom of that one soon.

For now, picking the dishes back up, “Who is punishment for? Dostoyevsky seemed to think it was for the
offender, something about Roskolnikoff’s soul? Kant apparently also thought it was for the offender, that
the state affirms the offender’s humanity by showing them respect through punishment.” A soapy plate sneaks
out of her hands back into the sink, but doesn't break. “Of course, if punishment is administered, it needs
to be by the state, measure and proportional, enough to deter others from the same offence”.

“Was hers measured and proportional, enough to deter future pickle disasters? Sure. The only difference is
that it just didn’t happen, but the state and I have to pretend it did. Petra feels like the consequences
she wanted were manifested, her ledger is closed. I feel mildly embarrassed telling everyone.” The dishes
were over, she dried her hands and took a few gulps of lukewarm tea that was sitting on the counter.

It was time to get out Tango’s nemesis, the vacuum cleaner. He scurried into the bathroom.

She could barely hear her own thoughts over the vacuum now. “Was the punishment that didn’t happen for
Petra, then? Sure seems like it. The apology, the conversation about what happened, the food, that was for
both of us.”

Finally, quiet. “Was there a problem with procedure here? Or is it working as expected? Or is it societal
attitudes about consequences that are off?”. A few more gulps of tea.

Tango slowly strolled up and sniffed his bowl.

"I know," she said. "Breakfast."

* * *
The legal pad was on the kitchen table where she'd left it after signing. She had not moved it. It had been
sitting there for three weeks like a procedural artefact awaiting filing, and she had been walking past it
every morning with her coffee and not picking it up.

She picked it up now, between breakfast and laundry, and wrote for eight minutes before the washing machine
finished its cycle and Tango went to investigate the sound.

Legal pad — first notes
Punishment as theater — works only because audience doesn't know it's theater. Parasitic on general belief
in punishment it is secretly replacing. Can this scale? Probably not. Does it need to? Not if you’re just
trying to make a point about society…

R. — punishment for the offender's soul. Victim incidental. The novel forgets her.
Maren's system — punishment for the victim's sense of justice. Offender almost incidental?? Not quite —
restitution requires the offender to do something real. Both present.

Kant: withholding punishment disrespects the offender as moral agent. But: I felt like the restitution did
that. I really felt remorseful, and my apology was genuine. I felt — ? Come back to this. Punishment as
communication of harm done (Duff) – I don’t think state violence makes for good communication of anything
that can improve society. Punishment as deterrent (People, Most) – if the crime is worth it, it’ll be
committed anyways.

She put the pad down and dealt with the laundry. Tango followed her to the bedroom, watched her fold things
with the air of a quality control inspector who has seen this process many times and has not yet found
cause for complaint.

* * *
Now for the “treat” of housework, as Lena called it. She grabbed her aesthetically pleasing watering can,
filled it up, and headed for her giant fern. Tango followed.

She again thought about Raskolnikov. She had read Crime and Punishment at twenty-two and found his
reasoning structurally embarrassing — full of gaps he'd papered over with intensity. He used society as a
justification and never once looked at it. The victim was a philosophical prop. The whole novel was about
what the transgression did to him, which was interesting, but it was a very particular kind of interesting
that required you to agree that the interior life of the transgressor was the morally significant thing,
and not focus on examination of the societal failing of creating the circumstances in which he was driven
to the life of crime in the first place.

Her case was the inverse. Her interior life had been, relative to everything else, fine. Inconvenienced.
Philosophically disrupted. But she had slept. She had eaten. She had continued to practice law with

She put the legal pad in her bag. Monday she had a case — straightforward, a procedural appeal, the

was designed to produce, and sitting, as she always sat, with the gap between those two things and what
lived in it.
