# Freedom of Action: Notes Toward a Goal-Correspondence Account

*Working notes. Companion to the causal-transparency account of free speech
([submission/free-speech/manuscript.md](submission/free-speech/manuscript.md)). The aim is to see whether
that account's structure generalizes: is free speech a special case of a more general condition on free
*action*, where the action happens to be an utterance and the "recipient" happens to be an interlocutor?*

## 1. The parallel to chase

The free speech paper's move was to take a taxonomy that is diagnostically useful but positively empty
(Lowe's three types of obstruction, which say what makes speech *unfree* but not what makes it *free*) and
supply the missing positive condition (causal-transparency: the pathway from mental state to utterance must
be recoverable by a competent interlocutor).

Freedom of action in general has exactly the same shape. Isaiah Berlin's negative liberty — freedom as
absence of interference by others — is diagnostically useful in the same way Lowe's Type 3 is: it tells you
that *something* is wrong when others block your path, but it says nothing positive about which of your own
goals you are free to pursue by your own action, absent any interference at all. "Free to do X" needs content
even holding interference fixed at zero, because the interesting cases are the ones where nobody stops you
and the action is still not free in the relevant sense — theft nobody catches, for instance.

So the target is a positive condition, call it the *goal-correspondence condition*, that plays the role
causal-transparency played for speech: given an agent S, a goal G, and a context C (resources, institutional
position, legal regime — the analogue of the topic-context pair), when is an action a taken toward G freely
performed?

## 2. The intuition on the table

The starting intuition (this is the one to test against cases): almost every substantive goal in a social
world touches other people. "More money," "a job," "a partner," "revenge," "safety" — pursuing any of these
displaces someone else's access to something. If infringement-on-others were sufficient to make a goal
unfree, almost nothing would be free, which is clearly wrong. So the condition can't be "G is free iff
pursuing it infringes on no one." It has to be something finer: some infringements don't count, and among
the ones that do, there's a question of whether *this* goal, honestly described, actually requires the
infringing part.

The proposed test: **a goal is free to pursue if it can be stated at a level of description that does not
build in a specific other party's non-consent as part of what would count as success.**

- "Steal $100 from Bob" cannot be restated to remove Bob's non-consent without changing what is being
  described. The object of the goal *is* Bob's money, taken without his say. There is no version of this
  goal that both preserves its content and drops the infringement.
- "Have $100 more" restates cleanly. Work, sale, gift, a bet, a raise — none of these routes requires anyone's
  non-consent as a condition of success. The desire behind "steal $100 from Bob" may in fact just be "have
  $100 more," in which case the agent had a free route available and chose the unfree one; or the desire may
  genuinely be *Bob's* money specifically (spite, a grudge, a claim they think they're owed), in which case
  there is no free restatement and the goal itself is not one the agent is free to pursue by that action.

This is doing real work, but it needs sharpening. Restatement can't be the *test* on its own, because it
invites a gerrymandering objection: "become starting quarterback" restates as "have athletic success," which
looks infringement-free, and by that route almost any zero-sum competitive goal launders itself. Something
has to explain why that restatement is fine — spoiler: it's fine at the *original* level of description
already, no laundering needed, see §4 — while the theft restatement is not optional but load-bearing.

## 3. Candidate formulations of the condition

Three ways to cash out the intuition, worth keeping separate since they trade off differently.

**Option A — Rights-boundary condition (binary prerequisite).** An action is freely performed iff it does
not cross another agent's claim-right (property, bodily integrity, contract, an enacted legal entitlement).
This is the cleanest formally: it mirrors the two-stage structure of the speech account exactly — a binary
prerequisite (crossing a right ⇒ automatically outside the free-action region, full stop, the way an
unrecoverable pathway is automatically outside free speech) followed by a *graded* condition within the
admissible region (cost of the capacity/channel conditions, see §5). The cost is that it pushes all the
philosophical weight onto "what counts as a claim-right," which is exactly the question the theft/money
example is trying to settle from the inside rather than presupposing from a prior rights theory. Useful as
the formal skeleton; probably not sufficient on its own.

**Option B — Marginal-infringement condition (no hard boundary).** Every action has *some* negative external
effect on somebody — using a resource, winning a competition, taking the last seat. Freedom of action is
inversely proportional to the *weighted* infringement caused (weighted by how much of a genuine claim, versus
a mere interest or preference, was set back), summed with the ordinary cost to the agent of the Type 0–2
conditions below. This dissolves the sharp legal/illegal binary into a continuous landscape and explains why
some infringements (jaywalking, minor property disputes) barely register while others (violence, theft) are
heavily weighted, without needing an a priori line. It's the action-theoretic analogue of the free-speech
paper's "opinion-dependence" point (§4.3 of the manuscript): freedom depends on the *content* of what's
pursued, not just the goal-context cell as such. Cost: without something like Option A underneath it, "weight
of a claim" is doing all the work and is left unanalyzed.

**Option C — Will-transparency condition (the leading candidate).** An action a taken toward G, with respect
to an affected party P, is freely performed iff a's *efficacy* in achieving G does not depend on P's
ignorance, inability to refuse, or overridden will. That is: would a still succeed if P knew exactly what was
happening and were fully able to refuse? If yes, a is free with respect to P. If a's success is causally
premised on P *not* being in that position — on deception, force, or a fait accompli P never had a chance to
block — a is unfree with respect to P.

- Theft: succeeds only because Bob doesn't know, or can't stop it, or is overridden. Fails.
- Fraud: succeeds only because the counterparty is deceived about what they're actually agreeing to. Fails.
- Winning a job over a rival applicant: does not depend on bypassing the rival's will — the rival was never
  owed a say in the outcome. Passes, and passes at the *original* description, no restatement needed.
- Coercing someone into a contract: from the coercer's side, success depends on overriding the other party's
  will. Fails — and note this recovers, for the coerced party, exactly the two-reading structure the free
  speech paper gives coercion (§4.3): either their agency is causally replaced, or it survives at a cost that
  drives their own freedom toward zero. Same phenomenon, general case.

This is the option worth featuring, because it gives an actual analogue of *recoverability* rather than just
relocating the rights question. In the speech account, the interlocutor must be able to reconstruct the
speaker's real intention from the utterance; success that depends on the interlocutor being unable to do that
(lying) is what gets excluded. Here, the affected party must be in a position to recognize and, if they
choose, refuse what's being done; success that depends on them being unable to do that is what gets excluded.
Both are instances of one more general shape: *an outcome achieved by a party P2 with respect to another party
P1 is free just in case P2's success does not depend on P1's epistemic or practical incapacitation.* Lying
incapacitates the interlocutor epistemically; theft and coercion incapacitate the victim practically (or
both, in fraud). This suggests free speech is not merely *analogous* to free action but is the special case
where P1's relevant capacity is recognition rather than refusal. Worth flagging as a real conjecture rather
than a settled point — the disanalogy is that speech's interlocutor is being asked to *believe* something
correctly, while action's affected party is being asked to *retain control* over something; these could turn
out not to be the same capacity under a more careful analysis.

Restatement (§2) is then not a separate test but a *proof technique* for Option C: you restate a goal to
check whether some route to it exists that doesn't route through a specific party's incapacitation. If such a
route exists, the agent had a free option and (if they took the infringing one instead) chose not to use it.
If no restatement severs the dependence on a specific party's incapacitation — "have sex with X" where X has
refused, "get even with X specifically" — the goal is unfree at every level of description, and there is no
laundering available. The quarterback case never needed restating because it never depended on the rival's
incapacitation in the first place; restatement only bites when the original description already smuggled in
somebody's overridden will.

## 4. Taxonomy of obstruction, ported from Lowe

The free speech paper's Types 0–2 are background conditions (capacity to form the thought, capacity to
determine the utterance's content, an open channel to the interlocutor); Type 3 is where the positive
condition does its work. The same layering seems to carry over:

- **Type 0 — goal-formation.** Can the agent even conceive of pursuing G? Adaptive preferences, a horizon
  narrowed by upbringing or ideology, or simply never having encountered the option are Type 0 obstructions —
  upstream of any question about whether pursuing G would be free. (Direct contact point with Sen/Nussbaum's
  capability approach, which is largely *about* this level and doesn't obviously distinguish it from Type 1.)
- **Type 1 — capacity.** Does the agent have the physical, cognitive, financial, or skill-based capacity to
  act toward G at all, independent of anyone else's response?
- **Type 2 — channel.** Is there an available causal pathway connecting the action to the outcome — market
  access, legal permission to enter a profession, physical infrastructure — again independent of the
  will-transparency question?
- **Type 3 — the goal-correspondence condition itself.** Given capacity and channel, does achieving G by the
  action actually taken depend on some other party's incapacitation (§3, Option C)? Graded by the cost of
  reaching G via a non-incapacitating route instead, exactly as speech's Type 3 is graded by the cost of
  making the pathway transparent.

Freedom of action, on this picture, is a field over (G, C) pairs rather than a global property of the agent —
same move as parametrizing free speech over (T, C).

## 5. Key cases

**Licensing and chilling effects.** A state doesn't ban entry into a profession; it just makes the legitimate
route (credentialing, fees, years of unpaid training) expensive enough that the black-market route becomes
the de facto option for many agents. This is structurally identical to censorship in the speech account: no
explicit prohibition on the *goal* (earn a living this way), just a cost imposed on the non-infringing
pathway that pushes behavior toward the infringing one. The Soviet-ballot case (manuscript §5.3 — a costless
option and a costly one, with the costly one legible by design) has an exact action-theoretic twin in any
regime that makes legal recourse so slow or expensive that self-help (which infringes) becomes the rational
choice.

**Necessity.** A starving person stealing bread. On Option C, this still fails will-transparency with respect
to the baker — the baker's refusal is still being overridden. What changes is that the cost of the
non-infringing route (starve, or find charity, or beg) has gone to the point where the distinction between
"free" and "compelled by circumstance" collapses, exactly as the speech account's degree scale collapses
Type-3 freedom toward zero as coercion cost rises without technically eliminating the pathway (manuscript
§4.3, the "coercion at gunpoint" discussion). Necessity doesn't make the theft free; it explains why almost no
cost was left on the legitimate side, which is a different claim from unfreedom.

**Fraud as the action-theoretic lie.** Fraud is deception used to obtain what honest disclosure would not
have obtained — an action whose apparent goal (as represented to the counterparty) diverges from its actual
goal, engineered to prevent the counterparty from recovering the real one. This is not just analogous to
lying; on the conjecture in §3 it may just *be* lying, applied to action instead of speech, which would
explain why fraud statutes and lying-with-a-victim already share a legal structure (see §6).

**Victimless "crimes."** Consensual drug use, gambling, sex work between adults, assisted dying — on the
will-transparency condition these are free: no one's incapacitation is a precondition of success. Their
prohibition, where it exists, is doing something other than protecting freedom of action — paternalism,
dignitarian concerns, aggregate externalities that don't reduce to any *identifiable* party's overridden will.
This is the direct structural echo of hate speech in the manuscript (§6.4): hate speech is free speech that
gets restricted on harm-to-target grounds orthogonal to the freedom question; victimless acts are free action
restricted on grounds orthogonal to the freedom-of-action question. Both categories are puzzling in exactly
the way a two-dimensional account predicts and a one-dimensional (harm-based) account doesn't: the persistent
argument over whether victimless crimes are rightly crimes at all is what you'd expect if "free" and
"legally punishable" are tracking two different things that usually, but not always, coincide.

## 6. Legal application and the Mill/Nozick/Berlin lineage

The manuscript's move on free speech law (§5.4) — a free speech law guarantees that legal cost in a specific
cell won't be raised above a threshold — has a criminal-law twin: property, contract, and tort law can be
read as guaranteeing that the cost of the *legitimate* route to ordinary goals (acquiring wealth, resolving
disputes, transferring goods) stays low enough that the will-transparent option remains the rational one, and
criminal law as the machinery that directly raises the cost of the infringing option to match.

The manuscript's two-part test for when law may reach an already-unfree lie (§6.3: provable falsity, plus an
identifiable victim) ports over almost unchanged: law reaches an already-unfree infringing action when (a)
the infringement is provable, and (b) there is an identifiable party whose will was overridden. This is
already, descriptively, how most criminal law is actually organized (the requirement of an identifiable
victim or a proxy for one, as opposed to prohibiting anything merely distasteful), which is either a good
sign for the account or a sign that it has smuggled in existing legal categories rather than deriving them —
worth being suspicious of this until it's pressure-tested against a case the law gets wrong by its own lights
(e.g. attempted crimes with no realized victim, or strict-liability regulatory offenses).

Antecedents worth situating against explicitly, on the model of the manuscript's §2:

- **Berlin's negative liberty** is the Lowe-taxonomy analogue: diagnostic, not positive. This account is meant
  to supply what Berlin leaves open, the way causal-transparency supplies what Lowe's Type 3 leaves open.
- **Mill's harm principle** is the direct rival, playing the role Mill's truth-argument plays for speech
  (manuscript §2.5): both put a substantive/consequentialist notion — harm — where this account wants a
  structural one — will-transparency. The payoff of the structural version, if it holds up, is a positive
  criterion for what counts as harm-that-matters (overridden will, not just any negative effect) rather than
  leaving "harm" as an unanalyzed primitive that has to do all the work in Mill's own framework.
- **Nozick's side constraints** are close kin to Option A (§3) almost by construction — a constraint against
  crossing others' rights, causally restated. The interesting question is whether Option C (will-transparency)
  is a *restatement* of Nozick's side constraints or a genuine competitor that happens to agree on the easy
  cases (theft, fraud, coercion) and diverges on harder ones (paternalistic restriction, positional goods,
  the necessity case above).

## 7. Objections to pressure-test

Mirroring the manuscript's §6 structure directly:

1. **Relational-property objection.** Freedom of action, on this account, is relative to *which* other party
   is at issue — an action can be free with respect to everyone except one specific victim. Is this a bug
   (freedom should be a monadic property of the agent) or, as the manuscript argues for speech (§6.1), the
   correct diagnosis of what we're actually interested in?
2. **Degrees objection.** Kramer's point that particular freedoms are binary and gradation belongs only to
   aggregation (manuscript §6.2) applies here just as directly: the atomic decision (act or don't) is binary;
   the degree talk lives in how the cost of the non-infringing route varies across the (G, C) landscape as G,
   the specific other party, or the context varies. Same dissolution of the apparent contradiction.
3. **Gerrymandering objection**, addressed in §3/§4 above but worth stating sharply as an objection in its own
   right: does the restatement move let any goal launder itself into freedom by climbing to a high enough
   level of abstraction? The answer on offer is that restatement is diagnostic, not constitutive — Option C
   is what does the actual work, and restatement only ever succeeds when the original goal didn't in fact
   depend on a specific party's incapacitation to begin with.
4. **Positional-goods objection**, not yet answered above. Some goods are inherently scarce in a way that
   makes "no one's will is overridden" too easy to satisfy while something that still feels like unfreedom is
   going on — e.g., an economy so unequal that only some agents can ever convert effort into the goods they
   want, without anyone's claim-right being crossed at any step. This looks like a genuine gap: Option C might
   be necessary but not sufficient, and something Type 1/2-shaped (capacity and channel, not the Type 3
   correspondence condition) may be carrying more of the real weight in these cases than the taxonomy in §4
   currently credits.

## 8. Open questions

- Is the will-transparency/recognition-incapacitation unification in §3 (Option C) actually one condition, or
  does it just look unified because "epistemic" and "practical" incapacitation happen to coincide in the easy
  cases (theft, fraud, coercion) and come apart somewhere not yet identified?
- What exactly is the baseline of "ambient" competitive displacement that doesn't count as infringement
  (§2's quarterback case, §7's positional-goods objection)? Needs something like a Hohfeldian claim-right/mere-
  interest distinction, but that distinction is itself contested, and the account would ideally generate it
  rather than import it.
- Does this generalize *downward* to make free speech a special case (§3's conjecture), or is the fit
  superficial — is "recognize a true belief" too different a capacity from "retain control over one's
  holdings/body" for the unification to survive scrutiny?
- The manuscript's inferential-propagation topology (§5.2: suppression in one topic-context cell spreads to
  inferentially adjacent cells) presumably has an action-theoretic analogue — does closing off one legitimate
  route to a goal make adjacent goals more expensive too? (Losing access to one profession via discriminatory
  licensing plausibly raises the cost of adjacent goals — savings, family formation — that depend on it.)
  Worth a full pass once the core condition is more settled.
