# Rights as Anticipatory Prohibitions: Law, Morality, and the Reducibility of Rights-Talk

*Working notes — key points from the June discussion. Draft skeleton, not yet a paper.*

## Core thesis

Rights and obligations are not a foundational moral category. They are rhetorically
and legally useful reframings of moral prohibitions on agents. At the foundational
moral level, all the content reduces to claims of the form: **agent X is prohibited
from doing (or failing to do) Y.** Rights-talk and obligation-talk are the same content
viewed from different angles — the victim's, the institution's, the member's — but
neither adds moral content the prohibition does not already carry.

This is a deflationary/reductive account in the same family as the other papers: as
[free-speech] reduces "free speech" to causal-transparency and [logic-morality] reduces
the demand for logical structure to a memetic fitness property, this reduces moral
rights to moral prohibitions, and explains rights-talk as borrowed legal-institutional
language. The non-cognitivist starting point of [logic-morality] applies directly: a
moral prohibition is the expression of a preference ("torturing is bad" ≈ "boo to
torture"), and a right is a re-description of that preference from the patient's side.

## 1 The reduction

Any moral right can be expressed as a moral judgement about the badness of an action:

- "right not to be tortured" → "torturing is bad"
- "right to free speech" → "silencing is wrong"
- "right to healthcare" → "denying care is bad"

The translation always goes through. The question is whether anything is lost.

## 2 What the rights-theorist says is lost — and why it isn't (morally)

The standing objection (Dworkin, Nozick, broadly Kantian) is that a right does
something a bare action-evaluation does not: it locates moral weight in the *person*
rather than in the *act*. Three features are alleged to survive only in rights-talk:

1. **Directedness.** "Don't torture" speaks to the torturer; "you have a right" specifies
   that *you*, the rightholder, have a claim against particular others. The prohibition is
   impersonal, the right is directed.
2. **Standing / remedy.** Rights-language implies the victim has standing to demand
   redress — to complain, protest, sue. A bare badness-judgement does not automatically
   generate that standing.
3. **Trumping / priority.** Rights function as trumps: they override competing
   considerations even when violating them would produce a better overall outcome.
   Expressing everything as badness risks collapsing back into weighing.

**The reply.** In a *moral* (non-institutional) context, none of these three does work the
prohibition wasn't already doing. There is no institution to receive the action-guidance,
no court to enforce the claim, no mechanism that makes the positive ("you have a right")
formulation do anything the prohibition ("don't") does not. The moral right *floats free*:
it neither guides action nor grounds judgement — all of that is done by the prohibition of
X, not by the positive right not to have X done to you.

The one genuine residue is **perspective shift**: the right addresses the patient rather
than the agent, and implicitly tells the patient they may resist, refuse, protest. But this
is motivational/rhetorical framing, not additional moral content. (Note: this is the same
agent/patient or illocutionary/perlocutionary split that does work in [free-speech] §6.3 and
[manipulation] — worth exploiting the parallel.)

## 3 Rights-talk is imported legal language

In *law*, rights are indispensable precisely because they guide institutional action:
they direct enforcement, ground punishment of violators, confer standing. The features
in §2 are real and load-bearing *there*, because the institution exists to receive them.

This suggests rights-talk is essentially **legal/political language** (Magna Carta,
constitutional design, human-rights law) that philosophers imported into ethics, where it
never had a proper home. So:

> **Moral rights are best understood as anticipatory legal claims** — moral arguments about
> what ought to be institutionalised, dressed in the language of the institution they are
> calling for.

This makes the framing genuinely *useful* rather than merely eliminable: reformulating a
moral prohibition as a right is the natural way to lay the foundation for a corresponding
legal right. The error is only in treating moral rights as a foundational moral category
rather than as prohibitions pre-formatted for legislation.

## 4 The hard case: positive-provision rights and the diffusion problem

Negative rights reduce cleanly (right not to be X'd = prohibition on X'ing). Positive
provision rights — healthcare, food, water, safety — are harder. The "right to healthcare"
requires a healthcare apparatus up to standard, but:

- **Legally**, the government is the obligated party.
- **Morally**, there is *no one*. No single individual is obligated to provide it.

If moral prohibitions always attach to agents, and there is no agent, the prohibition has
nothing to grip. Three framings, two of which fail:

1. *Prohibition on collective inaction* — "it is wrong for the group, as a collective, to
   fail to produce healthcare." Fails if collectives aren't moral agents: culpability
   diffuses into nobody.
2. *Distributed individual prohibition* — "each person is prohibited from failing to
   support structures that would provide it, where they have power to." Goes thin
   immediately: hard to say a random citizen wrongs anyone by not lobbying for reform.
3. *Legal framing as the only coherent one* — government can bear the obligation where no
   individual can, precisely because it is a **constructed agent** with defined
   responsibilities and enforcement mechanisms. It is an agent *designed to bear
   obligations no natural person could.*

## 5 Group agency dissolves the diffusion problem

Framing (3) is rescued by the literature on group agency:

- **Pettit & List, *Group Agency* (2011):** groups can be genuine agents with beliefs,
  intentions, and hence moral responsibility, *when* they have a decision-making structure
  producing outputs not reducible to individual members' views.
- **Margaret Gilbert — plural subjects / joint commitment:** groups acting from joint
  commitment generate genuine obligations between members, irreducible to individual
  obligations.
- **Collective intentionality (Bratman, Searle):** what makes an intention genuinely shared
  rather than merely parallel.
- **Toni Erskine — collective moral responsibility** in international contexts: directly
  relevant to the healthcare case.

The robust finding: groups can be moral agents, but **only when they have a structured
decision procedure operating independently of any single member** — which maps almost
exactly onto what a legal institution *is*.

**Resolution.** Only structured group agents — *and their subjects*, e.g. citizens obligated
to pay taxes that fund healthcare — can bear moral obligations to fulfil positive rights.
Membership in the group agent is what generates the concrete individual duty that did not
exist before. This solves the diffusion problem identified in §4: the obligation is not
free-floating; it is borne by the constructed agent and distributed to members through
membership.

## 6 Obligation vs. aspiration; morality as design brief

A tension to resolve carefully (flagged in the discussion as unresolved): in what sense is
a moral claim "real" if no agent is bound by it? If no capable agent exists, there is no
genuine moral *obligation* — only a **moral aspiration**: a statement that a certain state
of affairs would be good to bring about. This is meaningfully weaker than a prohibition or
obligation.

So for positive-provision rights, before the institution exists, morality can only say
*"this ought to be institutionalised."* It cannot generate a genuine prohibition on any
agent until law creates the agent capable of bearing it. **Morality here functions as a
design brief for legal institutions, not as a direct action-guide.**

## 7 The resulting picture of law–morality relations

Not "rights belong only to law" and not "law merely restates moral facts." Rather a cycle:

1. **Morality diagnoses** what ought to be protected and why.
2. **Law institutionalises** those protections and makes them enforceable — creating, where
   needed, the constructed agents capable of bearing positive obligations.
3. **Morality critiques** whether the law is doing its job correctly.

Law is therefore the mechanism that transforms moral judgements (prohibitions/aspirations)
into actual protections. Morality does not drop out, but its first-order content remains a
system of prohibitions on agents; "rights" and "obligations" are the interface vocabulary
between that system and the institutions built on top of it.

Note also the **thin/thick split**: individual moral obligations are relatively thin (don't
actively harm); the thick obligations (provide healthcare, safety, food) are structural and
fall on institutions, not individuals. This independently supports the §4–§5 conclusion.

## 8 Connection to existing papers

- **[logic-morality]** already closes on law and morality as twin coordination
  technologies for societies beyond Dunbar's threshold (law via external enforcement,
  morality via internal motivation). This draft is the natural complement: it explains the
  *content-level* relationship (prohibition → right → legal right) where that paper gives
  the *functional* one. The non-cognitivist base is shared.
- **[free-speech] / [manipulation]** supply the agent/patient (illocutionary/perlocutionary)
  distinction that §2's "perspective shift" residue relies on. Hate speech is restricted on
  grounds of harm to the target, "orthogonal to the freedom question" — structurally the
  same move as separating the prohibition (on the agent) from the right (of the patient).

## 9 Open questions / future work

- **The trumping feature (§2.3).** Does treating rights as trumps survive reduction, or is
  overridingness itself just a high-weight prohibition? [logic-morality] already treats
  overridingness as a selected fitness property of moral systems — can the same move retire
  "trumping" without remainder?
- **Reality of aspirations (§6).** Tighten the obligation/aspiration boundary. Is an
  aspiration with no bearer a moral claim at all, or a preference about world-states? The
  non-cognitivist answer: it is a preference, which is fine — but then "moral right" pre-
  institution is just a preference that a prohibition-bearing agent be constructed.
- **Group epistemology.** Raised but not developed: group agents have beliefs/knowledge, not
  just intentions. How does collective epistemic standing bear on collective obligation?
- **Directed duties literature.** Engage the Hohfeldian analysis of claim-rights and the
  "directed duties" debate (Darwall's second-person standpoint is the strongest opponent:
  it argues directedness is irreducible). This is the live target the paper must beat.
- **Is the reduction normative-eliminativist or merely descriptive?** As in [logic-morality],
  keep the project descriptive/metaethical: we are not saying rights-talk *should* be
  abandoned (it is pragmatically indispensable for law), only that it adds no foundational
  moral category.
