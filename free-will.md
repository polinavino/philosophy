## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 The Self as Controlled Hallucination

Anil Seth [1] has advanced the most influential neuroscientific account of conscious experience
as a form of controlled hallucination. On Seth's view, perception is not a passive recording of
external states but a predictive process: the brain generates hypotheses about the causes of
sensory signals and updates them against incoming data. What we experience as perception is
the brain's best guess constrained by evidence, not direct contact with the world. Seth extends
this account to the self: the experience of being a unified subject -- including the sense of
voluntary agency, bodily ownership, and temporal continuity -- is itself a set of predictions,
a collection of self-models the brain runs about its own states and capacities.

Seth distinguishes several components of selfhood: the bodily self, the perspectival self (the
sense of inhabiting a particular viewpoint), the volitional self (the experience of voluntary
action), and the narrative self (the autobiographical story one tells). All are constructed
inferences rather than direct readouts. The volitional self -- the experience of "I am the one
deciding this" -- is on Seth's account a perceptual hypothesis about the causal origin of one's
own actions, not a metaphysically privileged fact. Because all perception is a controlled
hallucination, the self is too.

The present paper targets this framing directly. "Hallucination" is not a neutral term: it carries
the presupposition that a correct percept exists against which the brain's model could be measured
and found wanting. For external objects this is coherent -- one can in principle compare the
brain's model of a chair with the chair. For the self, no such comparison is available. The
brain's model of itself is the only access point; there is no view from outside against which it
could be checked. Calling the self a hallucination -- controlled or otherwise -- is therefore a
category error, not a discovery.

### 2.2 Self-Model Theory

Thomas Metzinger's phenomenal self-model (PSM) theory [2, 3] develops a related account with
greater phenomenological detail. Metzinger identifies the self not as a substance but as a
process: the brain runs a PSM, a transparent, dynamic representation of the system as a whole.
Transparency here is a technical term: a model is transparent when its representational
character is invisible to the system running it -- the PSM feels like direct contact with a
self, not a model of one. Metzinger's central claim is that there is no self that has a PSM;
the PSM is all the self amounts to. Ego dissolution, depersonalisation, and the phenomenology
of out-of-body experiences are among the evidence he marshals for the PSM's constructed,
contingent character.

A feature of the PSM that bears directly on our argument is Metzinger's treatment of "mineness":
the property by which states feel like one's own. Metzinger ties mineness partly to the system's
representation of what it can intentionally control -- agency is structurally encoded in the
basic form of the self-model. This point is important and our account builds on it: agency is
not separable from the self-model, and a system that has lost its sense of agency has thereby
lost a constituent of its self. Where we depart is in Metzinger's continued use of illusion
language: the PSM creates, on his view, the illusion of a unified, enduring subject. The same
critique we apply to Seth applies here. Illusions require a contrast case. If the PSM is all
there is, calling it an illusion has no content.

### 2.3 Illusionism

Keith Frankish has labelled the broader family of views to which Seth and Metzinger belong
"illusionism" [4], using it specifically for theories of phenomenal consciousness. On illusionist
accounts, the distinctive qualitative properties of experience -- the "what it's like" -- do
not exist as they seem to the subject. The subject's sense that experience has these properties
is itself a brain-produced representation rather than a reliable guide to its underlying nature.
Frankish's account is not directly about the self, but it identifies the common structure shared
by Seth's and Metzinger's positions: in each case, the brain's self-directed representations are
claimed to be systematically misleading about their own nature. The present paper argues that
this claim requires a contrast case -- a correct representation against which the misleading one
could be measured -- and that no such case is available when the representations in question are
of the self by itself.

### 2.4 The Free Will Debate

Galen Strawson's Basic Argument [5] states the conclusion the present paper ultimately reaches,
though by a route Strawson does not take. For any action A, A was determined by the agent's
mental state M at the time. M was itself either caused by prior conditions -- in which case A is
ultimately traceable to factors outside the agent's control -- or it arose randomly. In neither
case does the agent bear ultimate responsibility in the sense required by libertarian free will.
The argument is valid and we accept it. What we deny is that it licenses hard incompatibilism --
the conclusion that genuine free will does not exist. We argue that it supports compatibilism
instead, once the correct account of the deliberating self is in place.

Benjamin Libet's experimental results [6] have been taken to provide empirical support for hard
incompatibilism. Libet found that the readiness potential -- a neural signal associated with
voluntary movement -- precedes subjects' reported awareness of intending to act by approximately
500 milliseconds. This has been widely interpreted as showing that the brain acts before "I" do,
undermining the causal role of conscious intention. We do not dispute the findings. We argue
that the interpretation depends on treating the deliberating "I" as something separate from the
neural process -- a move available only if one accepts the illusionist framing -- which the
present paper denies.

Saul Smilansky's free will illusionism [7] occupies a distinctive position in this landscape.
Smilansky accepts that libertarian free will does not exist and that compatibilism is real but
insufficient. His normative conclusion follows: the illusion of libertarian free will should be
maintained, since people could not live adequately with full awareness of the truth. The present
paper argues against this at the conceptual level rather than the normative one. Smilansky's
position presupposes that compatibilism offers at best a consolation prize and that what we have
without libertarian free will is a useful fiction. Once the illusionist framing of the self is
rejected, this presupposition falls away. The question is not how to manage a beneficial
illusion but whether the illusion framing was ever appropriate.

John Searle's account of free will [8] presents a structurally related bimodal framing.
Searle argues that all rational activity presupposes free will: deliberation is only coherent
if the outcome is genuinely open. He calls this the "gap" -- a phenomenological openness between
reasons and action -- and argues that it must either be illusory or grounded in neurobiological
indeterminism. He takes the second option seriously. The present paper agrees that rationality
presupposes something like free will, and that the gap is real. It denies, however, that
indeterminism fills it: indeterminism at the neural level just means randomness, which provides
no more agency than determinism does. The gap is filled by the causal structure of a
self-modelling system that runs its deliberations through reasons -- the compatibilist account.

### 2.5 Compatibilism

Harry Frankfurt's hierarchical theory [9] is the most influential compatibilist account of
what makes an action genuinely one's own. Frankfurt distinguishes first-order desires (desires
for actions) from second-order volitions (desires about which first-order desires one wishes to
act on). An agent acts freely when the desire that moves them to act coheres with a second-order
volition endorsing it -- when the internal structure of motivation is aligned across levels.
Freedom is not a matter of alternative possibilities but of the right relationship between
levels of desire. Frankfurt's approach is important for our purposes because it locates free will
in the internal organisation of agency rather than in the absence of prior causes. The present
paper builds in this direction: what makes deliberation free is a property of how the agent is
organised, not whether the causal chain extends beyond the agent. We depart from Frankfurt in
grounding the account in the structure of self-modelling rather than in hierarchical desire
alignment, and in deriving the result from a critique of the illusionist framing rather than
from phenomenology of action.

The compatibilist tradition holds that determinism is compatible with the kind of free will worth
having. John Martin Fischer and Mark Ravizza's reasons-responsiveness account [10] defines acting
freely in terms of the mechanism producing the action: an agent acts freely when that mechanism
is appropriately reasons-responsive, i.e. when it would produce different actions in response to
different reasons under counterfactual conditions. Fischer and Ravizza do not ground this in
self-model theory; the account is developed in the context of moral responsibility largely
independent of questions about the nature of the self. The present paper arrives at a compatible
conclusion -- the deliberating self is real and its deliberations causally efficacious -- by a
different route: via a critique of the illusionist framing that would otherwise license the hard
incompatibilist inference.

Daniel Dennett's compatibilism [11] takes a more deflationary form. Free will worth wanting just
is the capacity to deliberate and act on reasons, which humans plainly have. The libertarian
demand for something more -- a metaphysically uncaused cause standing outside the causal chain
-- is on Dennett's account incoherent, not merely unavailable. We share this diagnosis. Our
account diverges from Dennett's in the treatment of the self: Dennett's intentional stance
frames agent-talk as a useful interpretive posture that may or may not correspond to a deeper
reality. We make the stronger claim that the deliberating self is not merely useful but real --
because the concept of illusion cannot coherently be applied to the brain's model of itself.

### 2.6 Identity and Agency

The closest systematic treatment of the relationship between identity and agency is Christine
Korsgaard's *Self-Constitution* [12]. Korsgaard argues that actions constitute identity: we are
not pre-given selves who then act, but constitute ourselves as agents through choosing and
acting. The self is not a substrate that underwrites choices; it is produced by them. This is
formally similar to our view in tying the reality of the self to its role in action rather than
to any prior metaphysical substrate. Our account differs from Korsgaard's in two respects.
First, hers is a normative argument grounded in Kantian practical reason; ours is an argument
about the presuppositions of the neuroscientific framing. Second, Korsgaard does not engage
with the illusionist tradition and does not make the conceptual point that illusion requires a
contrast case. The present paper can be read as providing the philosophy-of-mind underpinning
for a conclusion similar to hers, reached from a different direction.
