title: "Logical Structure as Memetic Fitness: A Non-Cognitivist Account of Moral Consistency"
abstract: |
  We propose a non-cognitivist account of why moral systems converge on logical structure. The 
  mechanism is memetic selection at the scale where personal acquaintance fails: above the threshold 
  Dunbar identifies, cooperation between strangers requires moral codes that can be reconstructed from 
  partial information, applied without central adjudication, and used to make public commitments 
  credible. Logical structure, namely consistency, universalisability, and overridingness, is what 
  makes a moral code do these things efficiently. We integrate Sperber's epidemiology of cultural 
  representations with Dunbar's anthropology of religion to identify the specific selection 
  environment in which logical structure becomes fitness-decisive, then trace its implications. The 
  descriptive/normative dichotomy follows as a consequence: logical structure is not a normative 
  requirement on individual moral preferences but a selected feature of moral systems that succeed in 
  propagation. A corollary is that moral philosophers demanding logical coherence (a demand 
  belonging to ethics, the advocacy of moral codes, not to metaethics like the present account) are 
  participating in the propagation mechanism rather than uncovering normative structure. We offer a 
  new reading of the Frege-Geach problem along the way: rather than a defect non-cognitivists must 
  repair, it identifies a structural feature, the embedding of moral attitudes in deductive form, that 
  moral discourse both exploits for transmission and uses cooperatively to make moral preferences 
  publicly inspectable.

## 1 Introduction

Moral philosophers routinely demand that moral systems be logically consistent. A person who
holds that lying is wrong but also holds, without qualification, that one ought to lie to protect
an innocent person is typically told that they face a contradiction, a problem that calls for
resolution. The demand for consistency is treated as normatively binding: a moral system that
cannot resolve its internal tensions is defective. 
We argue against this treatment from the standpoint of a non-cognitivist and constructivist 
account of 
morality, which is by no means exempt from the consistency expectation. The non-cognitivist 
standpoint allows us to draw a parallel between our account of preferences (within
moral frameworks) and the treatment of 
preferences in rational choice theory underlying economics. Our 
standpoint-relative, constructivist commitment allows us to focus on 
reasoning about the logical structure of moral statements without making claims about  
their authority or the validity of their content.
In place of a normative justification, we propose
a fitness-based justification for the consistency of moral systems.

Moral systems with logical structure, i.e. those that are consistent, universalisable, 
and whose principles override competing preferences in a stable way, outcompete less structured
rivals in the cultural environment. They are easier to transmit, more capable of coordinating
behaviour across large and anonymous populations, and more resistant to degradation as they pass
between generations and communities. The demand for logical coherence in morality is best
understood as a memetic fitness advantage, not a normative constraint. 
In particular, abandoning the expectation of logical consistency would make moral
discourse impossible (or at least meaningless), since logical reasoning is the only 
tool we have for extending moral
commitments and justifying them to others. Abandoning consistency will not, however, 
absolve people of making choices and having opinions traditionally considered to be 
under the purview of moral discourse.

The account we develop is an interdisciplinary synthesis. It brings together non-cognitivist
metaethics, the epidemiology of cultural representations, the evolutionary anthropology of religion
and group size, and empirical moral psychology, disciplines that rarely engage one another, in
order to explain a phenomenon each touches only in part.

This paper makes three contributions:

- We show that the logical structure of moral systems serves three functional roles, each a
  selection pressure rather than a normative requirement: (1) making moral reasoning and discourse
  possible, (2) pre-commitment, fixing choices in advance so that behaviour stays predictable, and
  (3) social propagation, transmitting moral systems across persons and generations and coordinating
  strangers at scale (sections 3.3–3.4);

- We situate the account among the disciplines it draws on, to which it is largely complementary
  rather than opposed, filling the gap between findings each developed for a different purpose: we
  specify the transmission mechanism by integrating Sperber's epidemiology of representations with
  Dunbar's findings on group scaling (sections 3.5–3.6), and we explain value capture and the
  recurring pull toward utilitarianism as the cost side of the same selection pressure (section 3.8);

- We offer a new reading of the Frege-Geach problem: not a deficiency non-cognitivism must repair,
  but a structural feature moral discourse exploits for transmission and uses cooperatively to make
  moral preferences publicly inspectable, so deductive embedding is a fitness-enhancing pattern rather
  than evidence of semantic compositionality (section 4.1).

Here, it is important to make two clarifications. 
First, the three properties just named, consistency, universalisability, and overridingness, are 
together what we mean by the logical structure of a moral code. They let it behave like a deductive
system, a small stock of general principles from which verdicts on particular cases can be derived, 
which is what makes reasoning about the code possible in the first place. The kind of logical 
structure that confers a fitness advantage is not full 
formal consistency in the logician's sense. Real moral systems are never fully consistent and do not 
need to be. What matters is that there is enough apparent consistency to support inference to new 
cases and to let the code compress into a few principles rather than an open-ended list of situations, 
enough 
universalisability to apply across the members of a group without requiring case-by-case 
negotiation, and 
enough overridingness to resist being traded off against competing preferences in a way that 
would make 
the system unpredictable. When we later speak of compressibility (sections 3.3 and 3.8), 
we mean this consequence of consistency, since a consistent code collapses into a few principles 
where an inconsistent one cannot.

Second, the argument operates at two distinct levels and applies
different claims to each. The first is individual morality, i.e. the set of moral 
preferences a particular
person holds. This set can, in principle, be entirely arbitrary, locally inconsistent across cases, and
revisable. We make no claim that it must have logical structure. The only intrinsic constraint
is that an individual cannot reason about their own moral commitments deliberately without
treating them as if they had logical structure (as described by point (1) above), 
and the cost of refusing this is paid by the
individual alone.

The second level is society-level morality, which is the partly shared framework 
circulating in a population. It cannot be
specified accurately, since it is a heterogeneous distributed object: different individuals
understand the same norms differently, subscribe to them to different degrees, and apply them
inconsistently. Whatever moral framework propagates as a partly-shared social object is, however,
more likely to succeed in doing so if it has logical structure, even though a sufficiently powerful
transmission apparatus can carry a logically unwieldy one.

The two levels are not insulated from each other. Society-level morality exerts continuous
pressure on individual morality through the institutions in which individuals participate:
families, schools, workplaces, religious communities, legal systems, and media. Individual moral
preferences are therefore not only intrinsically free of logical-structure constraints but also
shaped, in practice, by participation in institutions that propagate logically-structured moral
frameworks. The arbitrary individual morality available in principle is rarely the individual
morality observed in practice.

The fitness claim of this paper concerns society-level morality directly, and individual
morality indirectly via this institutional channel (as well as via individual reasoning ability). 
The descriptive/normative dichotomy concerns
the societal level. What individuals experience as a normative requirement of logic on 
their moral preferences is, on our account, the downward pressure of this society-level structure, 
transmitted through their institutional and social participation, rather than a requirement 
holding of the preferences themselves. We draw out what this implies for moral philosophy
itself, and for the relation between law and morality, in section 3.7.

## 2 Related Work: Non-cognitivism and the Demand for Logical Structure

The metaethical tradition of non-cognitivism holds that moral statements do not express 
propositions with 
truth values, and therefore cannot be straightforwardly true or false. On 
Ayer's [-@ayer1936] emotivist account, 
moral statements express emotional attitudes, e.g. "killing is wrong" is closer to "boo to 
killing" than 
to a factual claim about killing. Stevenson [-@stevenson1944] refined this into a richer account 
of how moral language 
functions to influence attitudes in others, not merely to express one's own. Both share the core 
commitment that moral statements are not in the business of describing moral facts.

Hare's [-@hare1952] prescriptivism accepted this broadly but insisted that moral statements 
carry a logical 
constraint: universalisability. If I judge that I ought to do X in situation S, I am logically
committed 
to judging that anyone in a relevantly similar situation ought to do X. For Hare, this is not a 
contingent feature of moral discourse but a requirement built into the logic of "ought." We accept the 
non-cognitivist starting point but reject Hare's move. Universalisability, we will argue, is better 
understood as a fitness property of moral systems: 
one that helps them propagate and coordinate, rather than a logical requirement on moral statements.

The most coherent attempt to preserve logical structure for non-cognitivist moral discourse is 
Blackburn's [-@blackburn1984] quasi-realism. Blackburn starts from an expressivist base: moral
statements express 
attitudes. However, he argues that through a process of earning the right to talk as if moral 
statements 
were truth-apt, we can recover the logical behaviour of moral terms, including their embeddability in 
inferences. The Frege-Geach problem [-@geach1960; -@geach1965] concerns the difficulty a 
non-cognitivist faces 
in accounting for how a moral statement keeps a constant meaning when it passes from assertion into an
unasserted context like the antecedent of a conditional. The validity of moral inferences 
depends on this, and, on 
Blackburn's account this is a problem to be solved rather than dissolved.

We part ways with Blackburn here deliberately. The Frege-Geach problem is real, and we do not try to 
solve it. Moral statements are preferences, and preferences do not embed in logical inferences the way 
propositions do. The appearance that morality must be logically structured, the intuition that drives 
both Hare's universalisability constraint and Blackburn's quasi-realist project, is what we set out 
to explain, and our explanation will be memetic rather than 
normative. We return to the Frege-Geach problem in section 4.1.

Schroeder [-@schroeder2008] offers another approach to recovering logical inference for
non-cognitivists. His bifurcated attitude semantics assigns "being for" attitudes to act-types
and derives inference patterns from the logical structure of these attitudes rather than from
propositional content. Like Blackburn's, Schroeder's project is reconstructive, aiming to
vindicate the apparent logical behaviour of moral discourse from an expressivist base, and it
meets the same response we gave Blackburn: what both projects take as given, that moral discourse 
must support logical inference, is exactly what we take as the explanandum.

The closest ancestor of the present account is Gibbard's [-@gibbard1990] norm-expressivism. Gibbard
combines a non-cognitivist base, on which moral and normative judgements express states of accepting
systems of norms rather than beliefs about normative facts, with an explicitly evolutionary 
rationale: the human capacity for normative governance, and for the normative discussion that
coordinates action and emotion, is on his account an adaptation for social coordination. This
pairing of non-cognitivism with a coordination-first evolutionary story is the nearest precedent
for what we do, and our account inherits its spirit. We diverge on two points. First, Gibbard's
evolutionary explanation targets the purpose: why our species came to make normative judgements
and argue over them at all. Ours targets the cultural selection of moral systems: why the
frameworks that propagate across populations converge on consistency, universalisability, and
overridingness. These are different explanations at different levels, and the second does not follow
from the first. Second, Gibbard develops a logic of norm-acceptance precisely in order to vindicate
the consistency and embedding behaviour of normative judgement, treating that behaviour as
constitutive of the attitudes involved. In this respect he belongs with the reconstructive project
just set aside: the logical structure his semantics is built to deliver is, on our account, the
very thing to be explained rather than assumed.

Constructivists like Rawls [-@rawls1971] and 
Korsgaard [-@korsgaard1996] argue that moral norms are constructed rather than discovered in some 
mind-independent moral reality, which aligns with our commitments. However, Rawlsian construction 
yields principles that are genuinely normatively binding on rational agents that have chosen to 
hold them, and Korsgaard's 
constitutivism grounds moral obligations in the conditions of rational agency itself. For both, 
construction produces something with cognitive content and normative force. Our constructivism is 
non-cognitivist: moral systems are built rather than discovered, but what gets built are 
preferences, not facts. 

Nevertheless, our points of view share a desire for an agent to have their moral code 
propagate: to be adopted, acted upon, and transmitted across persons and generations. Where we 
differ is in thinking that this propagation ambition is prior to and independent of the logical 
structure they impose. The logical structure is recruited in service of propagation.
This applies even to constructivist moral codes that make non-imposition a central
virtue. A constructivist who holds that one ought not impose one's moral preferences on others
still, ideally, wants others to accept this meta-moral principle and adopt the view that
non-imposition is a virtue. In advocating for tolerance, the constructivist is propagating a
moral code that includes tolerance as a value. There is no exit from the memetic dynamic: even
a moral framework designed to resist imposition is itself subject to propagation pressures, and
the constructivist who holds it participates in those pressures whether or not they acknowledge
doing so.

A closer ally is Prinz [-@prinz2007], whose sentimentalist account holds that moral concepts are 
emotionally grounded responses calibrated by cultural learning rather than responses to 
mind-independent moral facts. Moral judgements, on Prinz's account, are culturally variable 
in content because the emotional responses they express are shaped by the social environment. 
This view, again, aligns with our non-cognitivist starting point. Where we go further is in 
explaining why moral systems, despite variable content, tend to converge on the same structural 
properties. Prinz's sentimentalism leaves this 
convergence unexplained. 

The closest contemporary methodological ally is Thomasson [-@thomasson2026], whose function-first
metaethics explains the features of moral discourse by the functions that discourse performs
rather than by correspondence to moral facts, and who reconstructs those functions through
empirical linguistics, preserving the grains of truth in expressivist, prescriptivist, and
descriptivist views. We share the function-first orientation but arrive at it from a different
direction and answer a different question. Thomasson traces how the several functions of moral
language are layered synchronically across its grammatical forms. 
We ask instead why one of those functions, transmission and coordination at scale, 
exerts over time a selection pressure that drives
moral systems to converge on logical structure. The two projects are complementary: a linguistic
analysis of what moral discourse now does, and a cultural-evolutionary account of why the systems
that survive propagation acquired that structure.

A cluster of naturalist accounts of morality lies close to ours and should be distinguished from it. 
Skyrms [-@skyrms1996] and Kitcher [-@kitcher2011] approach moral norms as evolved devices of social 
coordination. Skyrms shows that certain normative structures stabilise in populations through iterated 
game-theoretic dynamics, not because they are correct but because they are equilibria, so that no 
individual agent has reason to deviate unilaterally. Kitcher frames morality as a long-running 
collective human project of improving social coordination, with each generation inheriting and 
revising a moral code shaped by the problems their predecessors faced. Both accounts are compatible 
with ours, though neither draws the memetic fitness conclusion we draw.

Sterelny [-@sterelny2012; -@sterelny2021] develops a sustained naturalist account of how moral norms 
scaffold cooperation, emphasising apprentice learning and collective intentionality as the mechanisms 
by which moral norms are transmitted, applied, and revised across generations. Where Sterelny grounds 
moral norms in cooperation-scaffolding via the cognitive and social machinery available to small-scale 
human groups, we focus on the specific structural properties (consistency, universalisability, 
overridingness) of moral systems that succeed at the larger scale Dunbar identifies. These accounts 
are compatible but distinct. The fitness pressure for logical structure is, on our account, decisive 
precisely at the threshold where Sterelny's apprentice-learning mechanisms run out, which is the same 
threshold Dunbar identifies (section 3.6).

Curry's morality-as-cooperation theory [@curry2016; @curry2019] is a close relative that must be 
distinguished carefully. Curry argues that morality just is a collection of biologically and 
culturally evolved solutions to the recurrent problems of cooperation, namely kinship, mutualism, 
exchange, and the rest, and marshals cross-cultural evidence that these cooperative goods are 
everywhere treated as morally good. This is a thesis about the content and origin of moral norms: 
which norms exist, and why those and not others, while ours is about their structural form. The two 
claims are orthogonal and mutually supporting: if moral norms are cooperative solutions, then the 
coordination environment in which they must be transmitted is exactly the one in which, on our 
account, logical structure becomes fitness-decisive (section 3.6).

Girard's mimetic theory [@girard1977] offers a parallel account worth noting. Where Dunbar explains 
moral codes as coordination technology, Girard sees them as violence-containment technology, i.e. 
shared prohibitions that channel and redirect the mimetic rivalry that would otherwise tear 
communities apart. The two accounts are not in competition: a moral code can simultaneously reduce 
coordination costs and contain mimetic violence, and both functions favour the same structural 
properties of universalisability, consistency, and overridingness. Girard's account adds a further 
dimension to why these properties are fitness advantages: they are not only cheap to transmit but 
actively necessary for social stability.

A final interlocutor worth situating ourselves against is Parfit [-@parfit1984], whose project in 
Reasons and 
Persons uses game-theoretic structure to constrain which moral theories are viable. Parfit argues that 
any moral theory which is self-defeating, i.e. where agents following it produce worse outcomes by the 
theory's own lights than agents who don't, is thereby undermined as a candidate for the correct moral 
view. This is a sophisticated use of preference-ordering and collective action logic to do normative 
work: the structure of rational choice theory is imported into ethics to determine what morality must 
look like.

We find the analytical machinery illuminating but reject the normative ambition. Parfit is using 
game-theoretic structure to ask which moral systems are correct. We are using something structurally 
similar to ask which moral systems propagate and persist. Systems that are self-defeating in Parfit's 
sense are also likely to be poor competitors in a memetic selection environment. 
In our view, however, viability under collective action 
is a fitness property, not a requirement. More broadly, imposing a game-theoretic preference 
ordering on 
moral preferences assumes that moral preferences must behave like the preferences of rational 
agents in a 
formal decision theory, i.e. transitive, consistent, maximising. We abandon this assumption. Moral 
preferences are preferences, and like all preferences they can be revised, contextual, and locally 
inconsistent without anything having gone wrong.

## 3 The Argument

### 3.1 Where Logical Structure Actually Binds

Before arguing that morality does not require logical structure, we should be precise about where
logical structure does have genuine binding force. 
Formal systems with explicitly defined terms are governed by a specific logic from which new 
statements can be derived. In arithmetic, for instance, "1 + 1 = 2" is not an empirical 
discovery but a consequence of how the terms are defined. The binding force is entirely 
internal to the system. 

Descriptions of physical reality are different in kind. We cannot prove from first principles that 
reality conforms to any particular formal system. Instead, we observe patterns and construct 
mathematical
models to represent them. Those models can be tested and refined, and some achieve extraordinary 
accuracy, but they derive their authority from empirical fit rather than internal definitional 
necessity.

Moral statements have neither anchor. They are not formal variables defined into a system whose
internal logic then constrains what follows. And there are no moral facts readable off the laws of 
physics: moral claims are not 
falsifiable by observation of the world. 
The demand for logical consistency in morality therefore cannot borrow its authority
from either source. It must be justified on other grounds, or not at all. We argue it cannot be
justified as a normative requirement, and that the other grounds are entirely functional.

### 3.2 The Pragmatic Tension

If we fully admitted, at the level
of ordinary moral practice, that morality need not be logical, we would lose the ability to do
moral reasoning at all. Logical reasoning is the only cognitive tool we have for thinking carefully
about moral questions, including deriving judgements about novel cases from existing commitments,
identifying conflicts between moral attitudes, and publicly justifying moral positions to others.
A moral discourse that explicitly abandoned logical structure would collapse into an enumeration
of situation-specific preferences with no generative power.

This is not a refutation of our central claim. It is an illustration of how thoroughly the fitness
advantage of logical structure has shaped even our meta-level thinking about morality. We cannot
reason about morality without a framework, because the framework is the only reasoning tool available. 
The purpose of a logical moral framework is, therefore, 
for use in performing cognitive and social work that its structure makes possible:
extending moral attitudes to new cases, resolving apparent conflicts, and, above all, transmitting
moral commitments in a form that others can engage with, evaluate, and adopt. The pragmatic
usefulness of logical structure in these situations is one of the reasons for 
its propagative prowess, not evidence of its normative necessity.

A commonplace attitude in philosophy holds that one must follow one's premises wherever they
lead. Our account argues against this. The familiar transplant case makes the tension vivid: a
surgeon could save five dying patients by killing one healthy visitor and distributing their
organs, and the aggregate-welfare calculus that recommends this is notoriously difficult for
utilitarians to answer on their own premises. Almost no one accepts the conclusion, and our
account explains why they are not obliged to. A moral code that licensed such killings would
wreck the very coordination it exists to secure, since no one could trust a hospital. 
The revulsion that blocks the inference is not a lapse of rigour to be corrected by cleaner logic, 
but the
selection pressure doing its work. Following an argument to its conclusion is warranted only where the 
conclusion serves the propagative and coordinating function of the code. Where it would corrode
that function, declining to follow it is fidelity to what moral reasoning is for, not a failure of it.

### 3.3 The Three Functional Roles of Logical Structure

We now argue that logical structure in morality serves three distinct functional roles, ordered
by their scale of operation and by their dependence on one another. Each role explains why
consistency is useful without implying that it is normatively required. 

Role (1) is making moral reasoning and discourse possible (see section 3.2). Without
enough logical structure to support inference, there is no moral philosophy, no public moral
argument, and no way to extend moral attitudes to cases not yet encountered. This operates at the
level of individual cognition and is a precondition for the other two roles.

Role (2) is pre-commitment. Agents use consistent moral frameworks to decide in advance how they
would respond to situations they have not yet faced, which lowers the deliberative cost of novel
cases and gives others a stable basis for expectation. On a non-cognitivist reading this runs
parallel to the treatment of preferences in decision theory and rational choice: because moral
preferences are taken as given rather than as truth-apt, they behave like the preferences economics
models, where transitivity and completeness are imposed not because preferences are found to be
consistent but because a consistent ordering gives economics a solid non-contradictory model 
of human behaviour on which to build other research. 
Consistency there is an idealisation that earns its place by doing coordinating and
predictive work, not a normative truth about what anyone must prefer, and our claim about moral
preferences has the same form. The mistake is to import the requirement into morality normatively,
treating moral preferences as those of a rational agent in Savage's framework [@savage1954].
This is because pre-commitment can be violated. For example, someone who pre-commits to 
preferring A over C, having preferred A over B
and B over C, may still prefer C when the choice arrives, because the context shifted or they simply
changed their mind, and nothing has gone wrong.

Role (3), social propagation, is the most consequential of the three. A moral system compressible 
into a small set of principles outcompetes more loosely organised rivals in the cultural selection 
environment: it is cheaper to transmit, its violations generate visible contradictions that 
inconsistent 
codes do not, making defection easier to challenge and harder to ignore, and it makes the expressed 
attitudes of strangers predictable enough to lower the trust costs of cooperation across anonymous 
populations [@dunbar2022; @dunbar1996]. This role carries the main weight of the account, and its two 
halves are developed in the two sections that follow: why logically structured codes are cheap to 
reconstruct and resistant to degradation, the transmission mechanism, in section 3.5, and the 
threshold 
beyond which coordinating strangers makes that advantage decisive, the selection environment, in 
section 3.6.

### 3.4 Reasoning as Propagation, not Deliberation

The empirical case for our central claim, that moral reasoning serves propagation rather than
action-guidance, rests on two converging lines of evidence. The first is Mercier and Sperber's
account of reasoning [@mercier2017], developed below, on which reasoning evolved as a social faculty
for producing and evaluating arguments to persuade others, not as a tool for private deliberation or
truth-seeking. The second is Haidt's social intuitionist model [@haidt2001; @haidt2012], on which
moral judgements are reached by rapid intuitive processes and reasoning enters in their service.
Haidt's experimental evidence is the phenomenon of moral dumbfounding, where subjects presented with
harmless taboo scenarios, such as consensual sibling incest that harms no one, maintain firm
condemnation even after every reason they offer is answered, conceding that they cannot say why they
hold the judgement but holding it nonetheless [@haidtkoller1993; @haidtbjorklund2000].
The taboo example is illustrative of logic being the medium
through which preferences of any origin, whether emotional, instinctual, or evolutionary, enter
public moral discourse and become available for transmission. 

The conclusion to draw from this evidence, that logical moral reasoning is often done to support 
a previously made decision, is that the function of logical structure is, at the very least, not just 
action guidance. We argue that function is social and propagative, since reasoning is how moral
attitudes get articulated, defended, shared, and passed on, not only how they get applied.
This propagative role is what one
expects from a faculty that, on Mercier and Sperber's account, evolved for persuasion rather than
truth-tracking, and the post-hoc patterns Haidt documents are its signature.

The main qualification comes from Greene's dual-process account [@greene2001; @greene2013], on
which a slow deliberative system can override fast emotional intuitions in reflective conditions and
is genuinely action-guiding in some cases, particularly the novel or high-stakes dilemmas where
intuitions are absent or conflicting. Other work points the same way: moral-grammar research
attributes computational structure to fast judgement [@mikhail2011], Cushman's model-based and
model-free distinction leaves room for deliberation [@cushman2013], and Greene's own later work
softens his strongest version [@greene2014]. We need not resist any of this.

Our argument needs only the weaker claim, robust across that literature, that reasoning's primary
social function is propagation rather than private deliberation, since selection on logical structure
operates whether moral reasoning is post-hoc, deliberative, or both. Even in the novel cases Greene
emphasises, where no prior intuition fixes the answer and, for a non-cognitivist, no fact of the
matter settles it, what confers a judgement's moral standing in a community is adherence to logical
reasoning and the propagation of the resulting argument. This is why explicit reasoning is most
active precisely where behaviour is least fixed by prior intuition, in unfamiliar dilemmas, public
justification, and transmission across generations and groups, the contexts where propagation
matters most: in the absence of moral intuition, 
the logically consistent articulation the immediate persuasion task already demands is
both the basis for the moral decision and the very thing that lets a moral code travel.

### 3.5 The Selection Mechanism: Epidemiology and Cultural Attraction

The concept of the meme, introduced by Dawkins [-@dawkins1976] in The Selfish Gene, describes a unit 
of cultural 
transmission that propagates through imitation and communication. Like genes, memes are subject to 
selection pressures: those that are easier to transmit, more memorable, more generative, and more 
resistant to corruption tend to outcompete those that lack these properties. 
The more empirically developed
statement of that machinery is the dual-inheritance theory of Richerson and Boyd
[@richersonboyd2005], on which culture is a second inheritance system with population dynamics
of its own. We take the selection-pressure reasoning from that tradition without committing to
any particular unit of transmission.

Sperber's [-@sperber1996] epidemiology of representations offers a more precise framework for 
thinking about why some 
cultural items spread more successfully than others. On Sperber's account, cultural representations 
propagate when they are easy to reconstruct from partial information, fit well with existing cognitive 
dispositions, and generate further representations when processed. A moral system with logical 
structure 
has exactly these properties: its rules can be reconstructed from examples, they fit with the general 
human tendency to seek consistent frameworks for navigating the social world, and they generate 
new moral 
judgements when applied to novel situations. A moral system without logical structure 
requires something 
closer to memorisation of situation-specific rules. This is more expensive to transmit and more 
vulnerable to degradation across generations. This is the transmission mechanism named by role (3)
of section 3.3.

A methodological caveat is due here, because Sperber's own research tradition has moved away from
the Dawkinsian framing we have so far borrowed. Cultural attraction theory [@claidiere2014; 
@scottphillips2018] holds that cultural items are rarely copied with the fidelity a replicator
model requires: they are reconstructed anew by each recipient, and recur across a population
because transmission is biased toward stable end-points, or attractors, rather than because
high-fidelity copies are differentially selected. Our argument does not depend on the
replicator reading. What we have claimed is
that logically structured moral systems are cheap to reconstruct from partial information and
resistant to degradation in transmission. This is, in the theory's own terms, to say that
logical structure is an attractor: the form toward which moral systems are repeatedly
reconstructed, because it is what fragmentary exposure most readily and stably regenerates.
Claidière, Scott-Phillips, and Sperber [-@claidiere2014] show that the attraction and selection
frames coincide under specifiable conditions, so the descriptive/normative conclusion we draw does
not turn on the choice between them. We retain the fitness idiom for its familiarity, intending it
throughout to be neutral between selection among variants and convergence on an attractor.

### 3.6 The Selection Environment: Scaling and the Stranger Threshold

The most direct empirical grounding for our memetic fitness argument is Robin Dunbar's
anthropological work on the evolution of religion [@dunbar2022; @dunbar1996]. Dunbar argues that
religion evolved primarily as a group-cohesion technology, allowing cooperation in groups larger
than the roughly 150 stable relationships individual cognition can sustain (Dunbar's number).
Beyond that threshold, coordination requires external scaffolding, which religion supplies through
shared ritual and, crucially, its moral codes.

Dunbar distinguishes the religions of small hunter-gatherer communities, which bond members through
shared experience and lack formal moral codes, from the doctrinal religions that emerge as groups
grow larger, marked by a moralising high god, formal institutions, and a theologically justified
moral code that regulates an otherwise anonymous population. That code is central rather than
incidental, since a shared framework makes the behaviour and expressed attitudes of strangers
predictable enough to support cooperation. The predictability is only partial [@haidt2001], since
moral reasoning is often post-hoc rationalisation rather than a genuine guide to action, but it need
not be complete to do its coordinating work.

What Dunbar adds to the memetic framework of Dawkins and Sperber is the selection environment
itself. Below Dunbar's number, personal acquaintance, reputation, and the shared moral intuitions
Haidt documents [@haidt2001; @haidt2012] are enough to regulate behaviour. Above it, one must
cooperate with strangers, adjudicate disputes without shared history, and transmit norms to people
one will never meet, and only abstract, codified, universalisable rules can do this. The fitness
advantage of logical structure is therefore not uniform but becomes decisive 
precisely where the intimacy of small-group life gives way to the anonymity of large-scale
society. This is the selection environment that role (3) of section 3.3 names.

Norenzayan's [-@norenzayan2013] account in Big Gods provides direct evidence for the 
specific mechanism 
at work. Norenzayan argues that belief in moralising, omniscient, punishing deities enabled 
large-scale cooperation by making moral commitments credible to strangers. Where small-group 
religion relies on shared ritual and personal acquaintance to sustain social bonds, the 
moralising high god functions as a coordination technology: an agent believed to monitor 
behaviour universally and punish defection regardless of social proximity. The universalisability 
of the associated moral code and its applicability to strangers who cannot be monitored 
personally are crucial features of its social function. Norenzayan's mechanism converges closely
with Dunbar's work on religion and the scaling of human groups discussed above. 

This mechanism also explains why the institutional apparatus matters so much. Dunbar's account 
of how major
world religions propagated their moral codes points to the role of priestly hierarchies,
centralised bureaucracies, and formal rituals in sustaining transmission across generations 
[@dunbar2022].
A logically structured moral code can propagate further and more reliably with such an apparatus
behind it. Secular moral systems lack the church, the priesthood, and the ritual. What they have
are philosophy, education, legal institutions, workplaces, and media. None of these
carry the kind of binding authority that historical religious institutions did, particularly not
the credibility of supernatural monitoring that Norenzayan [-@norenzayan2013] argues was central 
to enforcing
moral commitments across strangers. In the absence of comparable institutional authority, logical
structure is what a secular moral meme has to rely on almost entirely.

The closest formal cousin to one strand of our account is the game-theoretic model of Lie-Panis,
Fitouchi, Baumard, and André [-@liepanis2026]. They ask why societies bind themselves to rigid moral 
rules, categorical obligations and prohibitions applied even where case-by-case reasoning would do 
better, and answer with an efficiency-robustness trade-off: because the reasons people
have for failing to cooperate are typically opaque to observers, a flexible norm that admits
excuses is open to exploitation, whereas a rigid norm forecloses the loophole at the cost of some
efficiency. Their model predicts rigidity precisely where flexibility is riskiest: in low-trust 
settings or among strangers. This bears on
our account in two ways. Its central prediction independently corroborates the threshold argument
we take from Dunbar, arriving at the strangers-versus-intimates boundary from formal first
principles rather than anthropology, and it supplies a strategic mechanism for one of our three
properties: overridingness, the refusal to trade a moral commitment off against competing
considerations, is on their analysis what makes a rule robust to disguised defection. 

Our model differs in
level and in scope. Their model selects equilibria among strategically rational agents inside a
cooperation game, while ours concerns the cultural selection, or attraction, of whole moral systems as
they propagate across persons and generations, and it assumes no such rationality of the carriers
(section 3.4 denies it). Where they explain rigidity, we treat overridingness as one of three
logical-structural properties, alongside consistency and universalisability, whose joint
convergence the transmission environment explains. Their result is thus a formal deepening of one
strand of the present account at the strategic level.

Henrich's work on the cultural evolution of moral norms [@henrich2020] supplies empirical grounding for
much of the propagation story. His findings on how the medieval Western Church's marriage and family 
rules, by dissolving extended kin networks over generations, reshaped the psychology of the 
populations 
subject to them (the WEIRD profile) bear directly on the institutional-apparatus argument above. 
They document a historical case in which a moral framework, enforced by a coordinating institution, 
restructured not merely the beliefs but the moral psychology of a whole population, which is the 
downward
institutional channel the introduction describes. We draw out what this means for moral 
philosophy itself in section 3.7.

### 3.7 Logic in Morality and its Upstream Causes

Naturalistic and evolutionary accounts of morality [@kitcher2011; @street2006; @joyce2006] have 
challenged the view that logic is a normative requirement.
They argued that moral systems are shaped by selection pressures rather than normative 
correspondence. What both normative and evolutionary accounts share, however, is an implicit 
conflation between what it means for a person to hold a moral commitment and what it means 
for them to hold a logically consistent one, as though rational agency and logical moral 
structure necessarily go together. Our account separates them. 

The demand for moral consistency is one of the most successful features of the secular moral 
meme. It is what secular moral systems rely on in the absence of the institutional apparatus 
that religious moral systems have historically used to ensure propagation 
[@dunbar2022; @dunbar1996; @girard1977].
This means that moral philosophers demanding logical coherence are participating in the propagation
mechanism rather than evaluating it from the outside.
Philosophy faculties, journals, and the tradition of moral argument are doing something functionally
analogous to what the church did for religious morality: maintaining, refining, and transmitting
a consistent moral code across generations. The tools are different: argument rather than ritual,
publication rather than sermon, but the function is the same. 

The same functional logic illuminates a familiar aspiration in legal and political philosophy: that
laws should, ideally, have some moral basis. Law and morality share the same underlying function:
both are coordination technologies for societies larger than personal acquaintance can sustain.
Law, like morality, makes the behaviour of strangers predictable and
enables cooperation without shared personal history. The two 
achieve this via different means, with law
using external enforcement where morality motivates people internally. 

Legal systems that ground
their authority in widely held moral preferences benefit from the same fitness advantages as morally
structured codes: they are cheaper to communicate, more resistant to defection, and more stable over
time than systems whose authority rests on enforcement alone. Where law diverges sharply from
prevailing moral preferences, compensating enforcement becomes necessary, costly, contested, and
fragile. The aspiration that law track morality is itself a product of selection pressure: legally
codified moral preferences propagate more efficiently than either bare legal rules or free-floating
moral attitudes, and the perceived moral legitimacy of law is among the most powerful amplifiers of
that propagation.

### 3.8 Value Capture and the Metric Form of Morality

The transmission advantages developed in sections 3.5 and 3.6 are one side of a trade-off, and a
complementary literature isolates the other: the cost of compression. C. Thi Nguyen's analysis of value
capture [@nguyen2024valuecapture] describes how an agent who enters an environment offering a 
simplified, legible metric
comes to adopt it in place of the richer, plural, often inarticulate values they began with, e.g.
citation counts stand in for intellectual contribution, step counts for health. A quantity
introduced to *represent* a value comes to *replace* it, because it is sharable, comparable, and
actionable in ways the value is not. This is the individual-scale version of Scott's observation
[@scott1998] that institutions rendering a population legible must flatten the tacit, case-specific
knowledge that does not survive aggregation. This is the trade-off our account locates at the heart 
of moral
propagation, seen from its cost side: the very properties that let a moral system propagate are what 
make a metric legible: compressibility into a few principles, universal application without 
case-by-case negotiation, and overridingness that resists local trade-offs. Overridingness in
particular is the moral analogue of a clear win condition, supplying what Nguyen calls value
clarity [@nguyen2020] at the price of the burden of a richer deliberation. A moral
code is thus among the oldest legibility regimes.

Utilitarianism is the limiting case here. Where most moral codes acquire the metric form by degrees, it
adopts it by design: a single scalar and an aggregation rule from which every verdict follows. This is
exactly Scott's administrative move of replacing a heterogeneous field of values with one summable
quantity. Its maximal clarity, compressibility, and decisiveness on novel cases are, on our account,
fitness advantages of the first order: a theory shaped like a metric is an exceptionally good meme.
But this explains its grip, not its content. The clarity that recommends it is the clarity Nguyen 
warns is seductive [@nguyen2021clarity], a property of the representation, selected for 
transmissibility, not a sign the representation is correct. Our framework predicts a maximally legible 
moral theory will
propagate especially well *whether or not* its verdicts track anything.

Nguyen does not hold that all metrics should be resisted. Rather, his remedy is to match a value's
form to its scale. In doing so, he keeps values thick and plural where that richness can be
sustained, but grants that a shared metric's benefits outweigh the costs of flattening wherever 
precise collective coordination is what matters. His example is reducing carbon emissions 
[@nguyen2024valuecapture].

We note that in practice, large-scale normative systems remain rich and pluralist to some degree:
they retain the richer form as an interpretive layer laid over the
compressed one. 
For example, in law, a broad principle is kept open and worked out through precedent, and
religious scholars dispute how deliberately vague texts should guide conduct. But this is no scalable 
substitute for the metric. It is invoked only where a principle buckles under a conflicting or
unprecedented case, and carried out by experts within a narrow field. The faithful form is
recoverable only at a cost, spent at the points where compression fails. This is why the concise
principle, commandment, or statute remains the default. 
The seduction of clarity is therefore not a contingent error which better hygiene would correct, but
the same selection pressure felt from the inside. In a broad sense, faithfulness is inversely
proportional to transmissibility.

## 4 Objections and Responses

### 4.1 The Frege-Geach Problem

The most immediate technical objection to any non-cognitivist position is the Frege-Geach problem
[@geach1960; @geach1965]. Moral statements appear not only asserted on their own but embedded in 
unasserted contexts, above all in the antecedents of conditionals. Ordinary reasoning treats 
inferences across the two as valid. Consider the inference: if killing is wrong, then getting one's 
brother to kill is wrong. Killing is wrong. Therefore getting one's brother to kill is wrong. For this 
modus ponens to be valid, "killing is wrong" must mean the same in the second premise, where it is 
asserted, as in the antecedent of the first, where it is only supposed and expresses no attitude. A 
non-cognitivist analyses the meaning of "killing is wrong" as the expression of an attitude of 
disapproval, yet no such attitude is expressed in that antecedent. The position therefore owes an 
account of how the statement keeps a constant meaning across its asserted and unasserted occurrences, 
and hence of why the inference is valid at all. Blackburn's quasi-realist programme [@blackburn1984; 
@blackburn1993] 
is largely motivated
by the desire to solve this problem, earning back the right to talk as if moral statements
behave logically, starting from an expressivist base.

We do not attempt to solve the Frege-Geach problem, and we do not think we need to. On our account 
the occurrence of "killing is wrong" in the antecedent expresses no attitude, so the inference does 
not carry anything from premises to conclusion the way a truth-preserving inference carries truth. 
Its apparent validity is not genuine semantic entailment, and we accept this. This is of a piece with 
a more general feature of moral commitment on our view, namely that consistency is not binding on it: 
someone can hold that killing is always wrong and also that they did it this one time and that this 
was morally OK, with no logical contradiction following, since a moral preference is not beholden to a 
specific logic. Rather than treating any of this as a deficiency that requires repair, we treat it as
reflecting the actual nature of moral statements.
This also means that the Frege-Geach problem, while real as a problem for quasi-realism, is not 
a problem for our position. It is a problem for anyone who wants to combine non-cognitivism with 
the semantic compositionality that logical inference requires. 

When a person embeds a moral attitude in a conditional argument of the form "if killing is wrong 
and this is killing, then this is wrong," they are not performing semantic compositionality. 
They are executing a rhetorical manoeuvre: borrowing the psychological authority of deductive 
form to make a moral attitude harder to resist. Our account explains why this pattern became 
so prevalent in moral discourse. Arguments structured as deductions are easier to transmit, 
harder to dismiss without explicit counter-argument, and more resistant to defection than bare 
attitude expressions. The Frege-Geach problem identifies what looks like a logical defect in 
non-cognitivism. On our account, it identifies a structural feature that moral discourse has 
learned to exploit.

This reading is not uncharitable to moral discourse, since the same deductive form has a
cooperative function alongside the exploit one: rendering a preference as "if killing is wrong and
this is killing, then this is wrong" makes the underlying commitments inspectable, so a competent
hearer can locate them, weigh them against their own, and challenge them at specific points rather
than merely be swayed. The embedding thus does two things at once, and which dominates depends on
whether it is used to foreclose disagreement or to enable it.

### 4.2 The Self-Undermining Worry

A reflexive objection presses on any account of this kind. If moral reasoning is, as we claim, a
faculty for persuasion and propagation rather than truth-tracking, then the reasoning in this paper
is open to the same diagnosis, and a reader has no more reason to accept it than any other
persuasive performance. The account would appear to undermine itself.

The first reply is that this misidentifies our project. The study of moral codes themselves and the 
action guidance they provide through logical reasoning and application thereof to specific situations 
presupposes the logical normativity of moral codes. The claim we defend is metaethical and
descriptive rather than prescriptive: it describes which moral attitudes people in fact hold, why
the ones that persist tend to be logically structured, for the reasons already given, and how those
attitudes bear on social life, how and when they are applied, and how they came to be. It does not
urge anyone to adopt a first-order moral code. What the account says about the propagation of moral
preferences therefore does not turn on itself, because the argument is not itself a bid to propagate
a moral preference.

A second reply reinforces the first. Even granting Mercier and Sperber's claim that reasoning
evolved as a faculty for persuasion, the modern development of formal and informal logic supplies
public standards against which an argument can be assessed with some independence from the
interests of whoever advances it. 
Mercier and Sperber themselves hold that argumentation is truth-conducive in just this dialogical
setting, where the production of arguments is biased but their evaluation by others is not. The
present account is offered in that spirit, as a claim to be assessed against shared premises about
cultural transmission and the scaling of cooperation, not as a bid to propagate a moral code.

### 4.3 Debunking and Relativism

A natural objection to any genealogical account of morality is that it debunks: if we can explain
why people hold moral beliefs by appeal to selection pressures rather than truth-tracking, we have
undermined their standing. Our position escapes this, and for the same reason in every version of
the worry. We are non-cognitivists: moral statements express preferences, not claims about moral
facts, and so are not truth-apt to begin with. A genealogy can debunk a belief, by showing it was
not produced by a truth-tracking process, but it cannot debunk a preference by that move, because
preferences were never in the business of tracking truth. Showing that a preference was shaped by
selection pressure leaves its standing as a preference entirely intact.

This is what separates us from the error theorists. Mackie [@mackie1977] and Joyce [@joyce2006] hold
that moral statements purport to describe objective moral facts that do not exist and are therefore
systematically false, but that charge lands only on a view that takes them to be truth-apt, and ours
does not. The non-cognitivist alternative we occupy is precisely the one Joyce's analysis makes room
for but declines. Street's [-@street2006] Darwinian dilemma is likewise aimed at realists, holding
that evolved evaluative attitudes either implausibly track independent moral facts or are severed
from moral truth altogether. 

The account also differs from Nietzsche's [-@nietzsche1887]. He took genealogy to loosen the grip of
values by exposing them as the sediment of power struggle and resentment. On our account, on the other
hand, it has no
such effect, since a preference shaped by propagation is still a genuine preference, worth holding
and acting on, and the history of how it got there does not change what it is. What genealogy leaves
open is not liberation but deliberate revision: we remain free to choose which preferences to keep,
which to adjust, and whether to seek consistency among them.

A related worry is that the account invites relativism (thereby undermining constructivism): 
if moral claims are preferences rather than
truths and the systems that survive are simply those that propagate, then goodness seems to reduce to
memetic fitness, with no standpoint from which one culture's morality is better than another's. But
fitness is not goodness. Selection favours transmissibility and in-group coordination, not the
welfare of everyone a system affects, and a coherent, universalisable code can spread while harming
outsiders, so nothing here licenses the inference from what propagates to what is good. What remains
available is comparative evaluation: the criteria one would use, welfare, coordination, and stability,
are constructed within moral cultures rather than read off an external standard, but from within any
such framework a system's effects are empirically assessable, and one that consistently produces
collapse tends not to persist. The evaluation is thus relative to a framework but not arbitrary, and
since the attitudes any new moral meme must fit were themselves shaped by earlier ones, there is no
neutral baseline to demand in its place. The relativist's observation that a morality fits the
culture that holds it is then not a rival thesis but one instance of the fitness mechanism at work:
how well a system fits the attitudes already in place is part of its memetic fitness (for which 
its logical structure matters, as we argue here). So, because
those attitudes differ from one culture to the next, so does what can propagate.

## 5 Conclusion

The argument of this paper is metaethical and descriptive in character. We have not argued that any
particular moral code is correct, or that morality ought to be logical, but that the logical
structure typically demanded of moral systems, its consistency, universalisability, and 
overridingness, is
best understood as a memetic fitness advantage, a property moral systems tend to acquire because
those that have it outcompete those that do not. That structure earns its place by doing three
things: it makes moral reasoning and discourse possible, it lets an agent and a community pre-commit
so that behaviour stays predictable, and it makes moral systems transmissible across persons and
generations while coordinating strangers at scale.

The account reaches this conclusion by an interdisciplinary synthesis. Mercier and Sperber's account
of reasoning as a social persuasion faculty supplies the mechanism; Dunbar's findings on the scaling
of human social groups identify the environment in which logical structure becomes fitness-decisive;
and the empirical moral psychology shows that reasoning typically follows judgement rather than
producing it, so that Hare's logical constraints reappear not as norms but as the structural
properties selection acts on. Logical structure is in this sense the machinery of moral transmission,
or more precisely a side-effect of the machinery of persuasion. Several things follow from this
vantage. Read alongside Scott on legibility and Nguyen on value capture, the same structure is 
what turns a
moral code into a legible metric, with utilitarianism as the limiting case: a morality in metric
form whose clarity is a fitness advantage rather than a mark of truth; and the Frege-Geach
problem reappears not as a semantic defect non-cognitivism must repair but as a structural pattern
moral discourse exploits for transmission and uses to make preferences publicly inspectable.

The account also explains a peculiarity of moral philosophy itself. Among systematic inquiries it is
unusual in being largely prescriptive rather than descriptive, advocating codes and telling people
how they ought to act. On our account this is what one would expect: the demand for consistency is a
move in the propagation of a moral system, not a discovery about a normative order (section 3.7). 
The prescriptive posture of ethics is therefore itself a
data point our descriptive metaethics explains, not a rival to it.

Several limitations deserve acknowledgement. The empirical work we have drawn on, namely Dunbar's 
anthropological data, the moral psychology literature, and Sperber's epidemiology of representations, 
is used here as theoretical support for a philosophical argument rather 
than as direct evidence for claims about secular moral propagation specifically. A fuller 
account would ideally be grounded in systematic sociological study: data on how secular 
moral frameworks propagate across populations, how their degree of logical structure 
correlates with their durability and reach, and how institutions such as schools, universities,
legal institutions, workplaces, and media mediate propagation in the absence of a
religious apparatus. 
Such work is methodologically demanding, since secular moral preferences are harder to identify and 
track empirically than religious affiliation or doctrinal commitment, but it represents the natural 
empirical development of the theoretical argument made here.

A further qualification concerns the scope of the account. It assumes a degree of 
homogeneity in the community of moral discourse that is not obtainable in practice. Different 
linguistic and cultural communities have different colloquial understandings of how logic 
applies to moral reasoning, and different starting axioms within their moral codes. Where these 
underlying frameworks diverge, whether across languages, cultural traditions, or forms of moral 
community, cross-community moral communication encounters a distinctive kind of 
friction. The moral meme must fit existing cognitive and discursive dispositions in order to 
propagate. Where those dispositions differ substantially, even a logically coherent moral 
system may fail to cross communal boundaries. The fitness advantage of logical structure is 
therefore context-dependent.

Three directions remain for future work. The first is that some moral memes appear to propagate
not only through the fitness mechanisms described above but also through cost-imposition that
specifically tracks resistance to the moral view. Doctrines of damnation for the unfaithful, 
shame and exclusion
attached to dissent, and guilt mechanisms tied to specific moral failures all display a
structural signature in which the cost to the target intensifies when they attempt to maintain
a contrary view and eases when they yield. This is the signature of manipulation rather than
mere transmission. The relationship between propagation pressure as analysed here and
manipulation as a specific propagation mechanism is a natural extension of the present account.

The second runs in the opposite direction to the core fitness story, and is worth study for the
tension it creates: logical strain in a moral framework does not always weaken it. Where holding a 
demanding, costly, or even internally contradictory belief is itself the price
of membership, the strain can bind adherents more tightly rather than driving them away, since
visibly paying that price is a hard-to-fake signal of commitment that outsiders and defectors will
not match. The escalating and increasingly counterintuitive doctrines that adherents of some
high-control groups are expected to hold as they rise through the ranks are an extreme case, in
which the very implausibility of the belief is what makes professing it a credible display of
loyalty, and the effort already sunk into the framework raises the cost of leaving. This suggests
that the pressure toward logical structure is not the only one operating. A rival pressure, toward
costly and identity-constituting commitment, can favour precisely the friction the first would
smooth away, and mapping the conditions under which each dominates is a natural extension of the
account.

The third concerns the extension of the moral domain rather than its structure. In this work,
we have taken the class of moral preferences as given. However, which conduct a community moralises, as
opposed to leaving it to taste, prudence, or convention, is itself variable and historically mobile:
intelligence, dress, food, beauty, and sexual conduct fall inside the moral domain in some cultures
and outside it in others, and the boundary moves within a single culture over time, as smoking and
meat-eating acquired the moral status that homosexuality and divorce lost [@rozin1999; @rozin1997].
The present account has resources to address this. Coordination-relevant conduct is moralised because
attaching universalisability and overridingness to it is what makes it coordination-effective, while
purity, dress, and dietary norms, coordination-irrelevant on their face, plausibly moralise as
group-identification markers whose policing sharpens the boundary of the cooperating unit
[@norenzayan2013; @henrich2020]. On this view the boundary of the moral domain would fall under the
same selection as its logical structure, its shifts through moralisation and demoralisation tracking
changes in the coordination or signalling value of the conduct in question, and the properties that
make a norm propagate would be the very marks by which a moralised value is distinguished from a bare
preference.
