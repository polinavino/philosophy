# Why Morality Doesn't Need to Be Logical and Why It Usually Is Anyway: A Non-Cognitivist Account of Moral Consistency as Memetic Fitness Rather than Normative Constraint

## Abstract 

We begin with a non-cognitivist premise: moral statements express preferences rather than 
truth-apt propositions. We argue that the logical structure typically demanded of moral systems 
is not a normative constraint but a memetic fitness advantage. Consistency, universality, and 
overridingness are properties that moral systems tend to acquire because systems with these 
properties outcompete systems without them at the level of individual cognition, cultural 
transmission, and social coordination. The relationship runs not from morality to logic but 
from logic to better morality: logical structure makes moral systems more transmissible, more 
capable of coordinating strangers, and more resistant to degradation across generations. A 
striking corollary is that moral philosophers demanding logical coherence are participating 
in the propagation mechanism rather than uncovering normative structure. We also note a 
pragmatic tension: fully admitting that morality need not be logical would make moral 
reasoning impossible, since logical reasoning is the only tool available for thinking 
carefully about moral questions at all.

## 1 Introduction

Moral philosophers routinely demand that moral systems be logically consistent. A person who
holds that lying is wrong but also holds, without qualification, that one ought to lie to protect
an innocent person is typically told that they face a contradiction — a problem that calls for
resolution. The demand for consistency is treated as normatively binding: a moral system that
cannot resolve its internal tensions is defective in a way that matters morally, not merely
practically.

This paper asks why. The question is less innocent than it looks. On a non-cognitivist account
of moral statements — the starting point we adopt — moral statements express preferences rather
than truth-apt propositions. A person who prefers not to lie but also prefers to protect the
innocent holds two preferences that conflict in a particular situation. But preferences can
conflict without logical contradiction, and there is no obvious logical rule that governs
preferences the way logic governs propositions. Why, then, does the demand for logical
consistency grip moral philosophy so firmly?

Our answer is that it should not be grounded in a normative requirement but in a selection
pressure. Moral systems with logical structure — those that are consistent, universalisable, and
whose principles override competing preferences in a stable way — outcompete less structured
rivals in the cultural environment. They are easier to transmit, more capable of coordinating
behaviour across large and anonymous populations, and more resistant to degradation as they pass
between generations and communities. The demand for logical coherence in morality is best
understood as a memetic fitness advantage, not a normative constraint. Logic makes for better
morality; morality does not require logic. We should note a tension this creates: fully
abandoning the expectation of logical consistency in moral discourse would make that discourse
nearly impossible, since logical reasoning is the only tool available for extending moral
commitments to novel cases and publicly justifying moral positions to others. This tension is not
a refutation of our account but one of its most striking illustrations — the pragmatic
indispensability of logical structure in moral reasoning is itself a consequence of the selection
pressures we describe.

This paper makes three contributions. First, we argue that the standard direction of explanation
runs backwards: the logical structure of moral systems is selected for by cultural transmission
pressures, not grounded in the nature of moral discourse itself. Second, we identify the specific
mechanism through which this advantage operates, by integrating Sperber's epidemiology of
representations with Dunbar's anthropological findings on the scaling of social groups: the
fitness advantage of logical structure becomes decisive precisely at the scale where personal
relationships can no longer sustain coordination and strangers must cooperate on the basis of
shared, codified norms. Third, we offer a new reading of the Frege-Geach problem: rather than a
deficiency that non-cognitivist accounts must repair, it identifies a structural feature that
moral discourse has learned to exploit — the embedding of moral attitudes in deductive form is a
rhetorical maneuver that increases transmissibility, not evidence of semantic compositionality.

The account draws on a non-cognitivist starting point in metaethics (§2.1), Dawkins' and
Sperber's frameworks for cultural transmission (§2.2), Dunbar's anthropological work on the
scaling of human social groups (§2.3), and the moral psychology literature on the post-hoc
character of moral reasoning (§2.4). The central argument (§3) applies these resources to the
three structural properties most consistently demanded of moral systems. We address the principal
objections in §4 — the Frege-Geach problem, the debunking worry, and the relativism objection —
and draw conclusions in §5.

### 2.1 Non-cognitivism, Expressivism, and the Demand for Logical Structure

The metaethical tradition of non-cognitivism holds that moral statements do not express propositions with 
truth values, and therefore cannot be straightforwardly true or false. On Ayer's [1] emotivist account, 
moral statements express emotional attitudes, e.g. "killing is wrong" is closer to "boo to killing" than 
to a factual claim about killing. Stevenson [2] refined this into a richer account of how moral language 
functions to influence attitudes in others, not merely to express one's own. Both share the core 
commitment that moral statements are not in the business of describing moral facts.

Hare's [3] prescriptivism accepted this broadly but insisted that moral statements carry a logical 
constraint: universalisability. If I judge that I ought to do X in situation S, I am logically committed 
to judging that anyone in a relevantly similar situation ought to do X. For Hare, this is not a 
contingent feature of moral discourse but a requirement built into the logic of "ought." We accept the 
non-cognitivist starting point but reject Hare's move. Universalisability, we will argue, is better 
understood as a fitness property of moral systems: 
one that helps them propagate and coordinate, rather than a logical requirement on moral statements.

The most sophisticated attempt to preserve logical structure for non-cognitivist moral discourse is 
Blackburn's [4] quasi-realism. Blackburn starts from an expressivist base: moral statements express 
attitudes. However, he argues that through a process of earning the right to talk as if moral statements 
were truth-apt, we can recover the logical behaviour of moral terms, including their embeddability in 
inferences. The Frege-Geach [17, 18] problem that non-cognitivist moral statements cannot embed in 
logical constructions like "if killing is wrong and this is killing, then this is wrong" is, on 
Blackburn's account, a problem to be solved rather than dissolved.

We part ways with Blackburn here deliberately. The Frege-Geach problem is real, and we do not try to 
solve it. Moral statements are preferences, and preferences do not embed in logical inferences the way 
propositions do. "Killing is wrong but not on Sundays" is a perfectly coherent moral preference, and no 
logical contradiction follows from holding it alongside other preferences. Rather than treating this as a 
deficiency to be repaired, we treat it as a feature that correctly reflects the nature of moral 
statements. The appearance that morality must be logically structured (the intuition that drives both 
Hare's universalisability constraint and Blackburn's quasi-realist project) is what we set out to 
explain. Our explanation will be memetic rather than normative.

We also depart from constructivist accounts of morality, notably Rawls [6] and 
Korsgaard [7]. Both argue that moral norms are constructed rather than discovered in some 
mind-independent moral reality, which might seem congenial to our view. But Rawlsian construction 
yields principles that are genuinely normatively binding on rational agents, and Korsgaard's 
constitutivism grounds moral obligations in the conditions of rational agency itself. For both, 
construction produces something with cognitive content and normative force. Our constructivism is 
weaker: moral systems are built rather than discovered, but what gets built are preferences, not 
facts. It is worth noting, however, that constructivists share with us a desire for their moral code 
to propagate: to be adopted, acted upon, and transmitted across persons and generations. Where we 
differ is in thinking that this propagation ambition is prior to and independent of the logical 
structure they impose. The logical structure is recruited in service of propagation, not the other 
way around. This observation sits at the heart of the argument that follows.

A closer ally is Prinz [26], whose sentimentalist account holds that moral concepts are 
emotionally grounded responses calibrated by cultural learning rather than responses to 
mind-independent moral facts. Moral judgements, on Prinz's account, are culturally variable 
in content because the emotional responses they express are shaped by the social environment. 
Our non-cognitivist starting point is congenial to this picture. Where we go further is in 
explaining why moral systems, despite variable content, tend to converge on the same structural 
properties: consistency, universality, and overridingness. Prinz's sentimentalism leaves this 
convergence unexplained. Our memetic account does not.

A final interlocutor worth situating ourselves against is Parfit [5], whose project in Reasons and 
Persons uses game-theoretic structure to constrain which moral theories are viable. Parfit argues that 
any moral theory which is self-defeating, i.e. where agents following it produce worse outcomes by the 
theory's own lights than agents who don't, is thereby undermined as a candidate for the correct moral 
view. This is a sophisticated use of preference-ordering and collective action logic to do normative 
work: the structure of rational choice theory is imported into ethics to determine what morality must 
look like.

We find the analytical machinery illuminating but reject the normative ambition. Parfit is using 
game-theoretic structure to ask which moral systems are correct. We are using something structurally 
similar to ask which moral systems propagate and persist. Systems that are self-defeating in Parfit's 
sense are also likely to be poor competitors in a memetic selection environment. These questions are not 
the same, and conflating them is precisely the move we want to resist. Viability under collective action 
is a fitness property, not a requirement. More broadly, imposing a game-theoretic preference ordering on 
moral preferences assumes that moral preferences must behave like the preferences of rational agents in a 
formal decision theory, i.e. transitive, consistent, maximising. We abandon this assumption. Moral 
preferences are preferences, and like all preferences they can be revised, contextual, and locally 
inconsistent without anything having gone wrong.

### 2.2 Memetics and Cultural Evolution

The concept of the meme, introduced by Dawkins [8] in The Selfish Gene, describes a unit of cultural 
transmission that propagates through imitation and communication. Like genes, memes are subject to 
selection pressures: those that are easier to transmit, more memorable, more generative, and more 
resistant to corruption tend to outcompete those that lack these properties. Dawkins' original framing 
was deliberately analogical -- memes behave like genes in a selection environment, without the claim that 
cultural transmission is biologically reducible. The analogy is sufficient for our purposes: we want the 
selection pressure machinery, not the biological reductionism.

Sperber's [9] epidemiology of representations offers a more precise framework for thinking about why some 
cultural items spread more successfully than others. On Sperber's account, cultural representations 
propagate when they are easy to reconstruct from partial information, fit well with existing cognitive 
dispositions, and generate further representations when processed. A moral system with logical structure 
has exactly these properties: its rules can be reconstructed from examples, they fit with the general 
human tendency to seek consistent frameworks for navigating the social world, and they generate new moral 
judgements when applied to novel situations. A moral system without logical structure requires something 
closer to memorisation of situation-specific rules. This is more expensive to transmit and more 
vulnerable to degradation across generations.

This gives us a precise sense in which logical structure is a fitness advantage for moral memes. It is 
not that inconsistent moral systems are impossible or even uncommon -- people hold locally inconsistent 
moral preferences all the time. It is that systems with enough logical structure to be 
compressible into a small set of principles from which further judgements can be derived will tend to 
outcompete more loosely organised rivals in the cultural selection environment, all else being equal. The 
advantage is real but not absolute: a sufficiently powerful transmission apparatus can propagate even a 
logically unwieldy moral system, as we will see when we turn to Dunbar. But in the absence of such an 
apparatus, logical structure is the primary resource a moral meme has to draw on.

The kind of logical structure that confers a fitness advantage is not full 
formal consistency in the logician's sense. Real moral systems are never fully consistent and do not 
need to be. What matters is that there is enough apparent consistency to support inference to new cases, 
enough 
universality to apply across the members of a group without requiring case by case negotiation, and 
enough overridingness to resist being traded off against competing preferences in a way that would make 
the system unpredictable. These are the properties Hare tried to derive as logical requirements on moral 
statements. We are saying they are selection pressures on moral memes. The outcome looks similar: moral 
systems do tend to have these properties. The explanation, 
however, is entirely different.

### 2.3 Dunbar, Religion, and the Scaling of Moral Systems

The most direct empirical grounding for our memetic fitness argument comes from Robin Dunbar's 
anthropological work on the evolution of religion [14, 15]. Dunbar's central finding is that religion 
evolved primarily as a group cohesion technology, i.e. a set of mechanisms that allowed humans to live 
and cooperate in groups larger than would otherwise be possible given the cognitive constraints on 
maintaining stable social relationships. His proposal that humans can maintain stable relationships 
with roughly 150 individuals (Dunbar's number) establishes a baseline beyond which social coordination 
requires external scaffolding. Religion, with its shared rituals, theological belief systems, and 
crucially its moral codes, provided that scaffolding.

Dunbar distinguishes between the religions of small hunter-gatherer communities, which are largely about 
communal bonding through shared experience and lack formal moral codes justified by religious principles, 
and the doctrinal religions that emerge as civic units grow larger. The latter are characterised by a 
moralising high god, formal institutional structures, and (most relevant for our purposes) a theologically
justified moral system that regulates behaviour across a large and otherwise anonymous population. The 
moral code is not incidental to the religion's social function; it is central to it. A shared moral 
framework makes the behaviour and at least the expressed attitudes of strangers more predictable, which is 
a precondition for large-scale cooperation. We note, however, that this predictability is partial at best 
(Haidt [10]), and people frequently do not act on their stated moral convictions, since moral reasoning is 
typically post-hoc rationalisation of behaviour driven by intuition and social context rather than a 
genuine guide to action. The moral code makes attitudes more legible, not behaviour fully predictable.

Norenzayan's [27] account in Big Gods provides direct evidence for the specific mechanism 
at work. Norenzayan argues that belief in moralising, omniscient, punishing deities enabled 
large-scale cooperation by making moral commitments credible to strangers. Where small-group 
religion relies on shared ritual and personal acquaintance to sustain social bonds, the 
moralising high god functions as a coordination technology: an agent believed to monitor 
behaviour universally and punish defection regardless of social proximity. The universality 
of the associated moral code and its applicability to strangers who cannot be monitored 
personally are not incidental features of theology but constitutive of its social function.

What Dunbar's account adds to the pure memetic framework of Dawkins and Sperber is an explanation of the 
selection environment. Moral memes with logical structure (universalisable, consistent, derivatively 
generative) outcompete rivals not in the abstract but in a specific context: the coordination problem 
faced by groups growing beyond the scale at which personal relationships can do the work of social trust. 
Logical structure solves this problem cheaply. A consistent moral code can be transmitted to strangers, 
applied to novel situations without central adjudication, and used to make public commitments credible. An 
inconsistent, situation-specific set of moral preferences cannot do any of these things efficiently.

Below Dunbar's number, the coordination problem is solved without formal moral codes. Within 
a community of roughly 150, personal acquaintance, reputation tracking, and the moral intuitions 
that Haidt documents [10, 20] as the primary drivers of everyday moral judgement are sufficient 
to regulate behaviour. Beyond this threshold, one must cooperate with strangers, adjudicate 
disputes without shared personal history, and transmit norms to people one will never meet. 
At this scale, intuition-based coordination runs out, and abstract, codified, universalisable 
rules become necessary. The fitness advantage of logical structure is therefore not uniform: it 
becomes decisive precisely where the intimacy of small-group life gives way to the anonymity of 
large-scale society.

This also explains why the institutional apparatus matters so much. Dunbar's account of how major world 
religions propagated their moral codes points to the role of priestly hierarchies, centralised 
bureaucracies, and formal rituals in sustaining transmission across generations [14]. A logically 
structured moral code can propagate further and more reliably with such an apparatus behind it. 
However, in the absence of that apparatus, logical structure is what a moral meme has to rely on almost 
entirely. Secular moral systems lack the church, the priesthood, and the ritual. What they have is 
philosophy, education, and the internal generative power of a consistent framework. This is why, as we 
argue in section 3.5, moral philosophers demanding logical coherence are doing something functionally 
analogous to what religious institutions did for doctrinal morality.

Skyrms [11] and Kitcher [12] offer complementary perspectives on the same phenomenon from within analytic 
philosophy. Skyrms shows that certain normative structures stabilise in populations through iterated 
game-theoretic dynamics, not because they are correct but because they are equilibria -- no individual 
agent has reason to deviate unilaterally. Kitcher frames morality as a long-running collective human 
project of improving social coordination, with each generation inheriting and revising a moral code shaped 
by the problems their predecessors faced. Both accounts are compatible with ours, though neither draws the 
memetic conclusion we draw: that the logical structure of moral systems is itself a fitness property 
selected for by the coordination environment, rather than a normative requirement imposed from outside.

Girard's mimetic theory [16] offers a parallel account worth briefly noting. Where Dunbar explains moral 
codes as coordination technology, Girard sees them as violence-containment technology -- shared 
prohibitions that channel and redirect the mimetic rivalry that would otherwise tear communities apart. 
The two accounts are not in competition: a moral code can simultaneously reduce coordination costs and 
contain mimetic violence, and both functions favour the same structural properties of universality, 
consistency, and overridingness. Girard's account adds a further dimension to why these properties are 
fitness advantages -- they are not only cheap to transmit but actively necessary for social stability.

### 2.4 Moral Psychology and the Primacy of Intuition

The empirical case for our central claim -- that moral reasoning serves propagation rather than
action-guidance -- rests most directly on Jonathan Haidt's social intuitionist model of moral
judgement [10]. Haidt's programme of research demonstrates that moral reasoning is, in the
overwhelming majority of cases, post-hoc rationalisation of judgements that were reached by rapid,
automatic intuitive processes. In his influential dumbfounding experiments, subjects maintained firm
moral judgements even when every rational objection they raised was systematically answered,
eventually conceding that they could not articulate why they held the judgement but holding it
nonetheless. The reasoning did not produce the judgement; the judgement came first, and the
reasoning followed in its service.

This has a direct implication for the role of logical structure in morality. If moral reasoning
were primarily a guide to action -- if agents consulted their moral framework, derived a judgement,
and acted on it -- then logical consistency would matter for behavioural reasons. Inconsistent moral
frameworks would produce erratic guidance and therefore erratic behaviour. But if moral reasoning is
primarily post-hoc, then consistency cannot be doing this work. The behaviour has already happened,
or the intuitive judgement has already been reached, before the reasoning begins. Logical structure
must therefore be serving a different function, and we argue that function is social and propagative:
moral reasoning is how moral attitudes get articulated, defended, shared, and passed on, not how
they get applied.

This conclusion is strongly supported by Mercier and Sperber's evolutionary account of reasoning
itself [24]. They argue that reasoning did not evolve primarily as a tool for individual
truth-seeking or private deliberation, but as a social faculty for producing and evaluating
arguments in order to persuade others and navigate cooperative social situations. On this account,
the propagative function of moral reasoning is not an accidental feature or a correctable bias:
it is what reasoning evolved to do. The post-hoc character of moral reasoning that Haidt documents
empirically is, on Mercier and Sperber's account, exactly what we should expect from a faculty
shaped by selection pressure for persuasion rather than truth-tracking.

Greene's dual-process account of moral psychology [20] offers a complementary perspective. Greene
argues that moral judgements arise from two systems: a fast, automatic, emotional system that
generates strong intuitive responses, and a slower, deliberative, reasoning system that can override
intuitions in sufficiently reflective conditions. On Greene's account, the deliberative system is
genuinely action-guiding in some cases, particularly in conditions of calm reflection on unfamiliar
dilemmas. This is a partial concession to the pre-commitment function we discuss in section 3.3
-- logical moral reasoning does sometimes shape what people decide to do, particularly in novel or
high-stakes situations where intuitions are absent or conflicting. But this is the exception rather
than the rule. For the vast majority of moral judgements in everyday life, the intuitive system
dominates, and reasoning serves rationalisation.

Taken together, Haidt and Greene support a picture in which explicit moral reasoning -- the kind
that requires and rewards logical consistency -- operates at the margins of actual moral behaviour.
It is most active precisely where behaviour is least determined: in unfamiliar dilemmas, in public
justification of positions to others, and in the transmission of moral attitudes across generations
and social groups. These are exactly the contexts where propagation matters most. A moral framework
that can generate consistent, universalisable, publicly defensible reasoning about novel cases is a
framework that travels well -- across persons, communities, and time. The logical structure is not
what drives the moral intuitions, but it is what makes them exportable.

If moral reasoning were primarily action-guiding, philosophers demanding consistency would be
helping agents act better. If it is primarily propagative, they are helping moral attitudes
spread more effectively. On our account, the philosophical demand for logical coherence is 
best understood as a transmission mechanism rather than the uncovering of a normative 
constraint. We develop this further in section 3.5.

## 3. The Argument

### 3.1 Where Logical Structure Actually Binds

Before arguing that morality does not require logical structure, we should be precise about where
logical structure does have genuine binding force. 
Formal systems with explicitly defined terms are governed by a specific logic from which new 
statements can be derived. In arithmetic, for instance, "1 + 1 = 2" is not an empirical 
discovery but a consequence of how the terms are defined. The binding force is entirely 
internal to the system. It is not imposed from outside but comes with the decision to use 
the system at all.

Descriptions of physical reality are different in kind. We cannot prove from first principles that 
reality conforms to any particular formal system. Instead, we observe patterns and construct mathematical
models to represent them. Those models can be tested and refined, and some achieve extraordinary 
accuracy, but they derive their authority from empirical fit rather than internal definitional necessity.

Moral statements have neither anchor. They are not formal variables defined into a system whose
internal logic then constrains what follows. And there are no moral facts readable off the laws of 
physics: moral claims are not 
falsifiable by observation of the world. 
The demand for logical consistency in morality therefore cannot borrow its authority
from either source. It must be justified on other grounds, or not at all. We argue it cannot be
justified as a normative requirement, and that the other grounds are entirely functional.

### 3.2 The Pragmatic Tension

There is an important tension to acknowledge before proceeding. If we fully admitted, at the level
of first-order moral practice, that morality need not be logical, we would lose the ability to do
moral reasoning at all. Logical reasoning is the only cognitive tool we have for thinking carefully
about moral questions, including deriving judgements about novel cases from existing commitments,
identifying conflicts between moral attitudes, and publicly justifying moral positions to others.
A moral discourse that explicitly abandoned logical structure would collapse into an enumeration
of situation-specific preferences with no generative power.

This is not a refutation of our central claim. It is an illustration of how thoroughly the fitness
advantage of logical structure has shaped even our meta-level thinking about morality. We cannot
step outside the logical framework to reason about morality without a framework, because the
framework is the only reasoning tool available. The pragmatic necessity of treating morality as
logical is itself a consequence of the selection pressures we describe. 

This observation does, however, tell us what moral reasoning is actually for. It is not primarily
for deriving correct moral conclusions from true moral premises. In fact, on our account, there are no
such things. It is for doing the cognitive and social work that logical structure makes possible:
extending moral attitudes to new cases, resolving apparent conflicts, and above all transmitting
moral commitments in a form that others can engage with, evaluate, and adopt. The pragmatic
necessity of logical structure in moral reasoning is a symptom of its propagative function, not
evidence of its normative necessity.

### 3.3 The Three Functional Roles of Consistency

We now argue that logical consistency in morality serves three distinct functional roles, ordered
by their scale of operation and by their dependence on one another. Each role explains why
consistency is useful without implying that it is normatively required.

The first and most basic role is enabling moral reasoning at all, as described in Section 3.2. Without
enough logical structure to support inference, there is no moral philosophy, no public moral
argument, and no way to extend moral attitudes to cases not yet encountered. This operates at the
level of individual cognition and is a precondition for the other two roles.

The second role is pre-commitment. Agents use consistent moral frameworks to decide in advance
how they would feel about and respond to situations they have not yet faced. This is a genuinely
useful cognitive function -- it reduces the deliberative cost of novel moral situations and
provides a stable basis for social commitments and expectations. It draws on the formal
decision-theoretic insight that consistent preference orderings are easier to act on than
inconsistent ones [23]. But we should be careful about how strong a claim this supports.
Savage's axioms for subjective expected utility [23] require that an agent's preferences be
transitive, complete, and consistent across contexts. This has genuine binding force within the
formal system because the terms are defined into it. The mistake is importing this requirement
into morality and treating moral preferences as if they were the preferences of a rational agent
in Savage's framework. They are not. A moral preference that would be inconsistent by 
decision-theoretic standards violates no axiom that applies to moral preferences, because no 
such axiom governs them outside the formal system.

Moreover, the pre-commitment is always defeasible: an agent who pre-commits to preferring A over
C on the basis of preferring A over B and B over C is entirely within their rights to prefer C
when the actual choice arrives. The preference landscape shifted, the context made C salient in
ways the abstract reasoning did not capture, or they simply changed their mind. Nothing has
gone wrong.

The third and most consequential role is social propagation. A moral system with enough logical
structure to be compressible into a small set of principles from which further judgements can be
derived will tend to outcompete more loosely organised rivals in the cultural selection
environment. It is cheaper to transmit -- a consistent framework can be reconstructed from
partial information and applied to novel cases without central coordination. Violations of a consistent 
code generate visible contradictions that inconsistent codes do 
not, making defection easier to challenge and harder to ignore. And it supports larger-scale social 
coordination by making the
expressed attitudes of strangers more predictable, reducing the trust costs of cooperation
across anonymous populations [14, 15]. This is the role that Dunbar's anthropological evidence
bears most directly on: the moral systems that enabled the scaling of human social groups were
precisely those with the structural properties that make propagation efficient. This is also 
consistent with Mercier and Sperber's account [24] of reasoning as a faculty 
shaped primarily for persuasion, discussed further in section 3.4.

These three roles are ordered by dependence as well as scale. The first is a condition of
possibility for the second, and the second amplifies the third: a moral framework that cannot
be reasoned about cannot be pre-committed to, and a framework that cannot be pre-committed to
cannot generate the stable, predictable, publicly articulable moral attitudes that efficient
propagation requires.

### 3.4 The Propagation-Over-Deliberation Claim

The propagation account gains its sharpest support from what moral reasoning actually does. 
As documented in section 2.4, explicit moral reasoning is in the overwhelming majority of cases 
post-hoc rationalisation of judgements already reached by intuition. Logical consistency therefore 
cannot be guiding action in any direct sense. What it does instead is exactly what the propagation 
account predicts: it makes moral attitudes articulable, defensible, and transmissible. When we reason 
explicitly about morality, we are almost always justifying a position, extending a commitment to an 
unfamiliar case, or teaching a moral attitude. All of these are propagation activities. The logical 
structure of morality is the machinery of moral transmission, not moral action. That this is not 
accidental follows from Mercier and Sperber's account [24]: reasoning evolved as a persuasion tool, 
and moral philosophy deploys it for precisely the purpose it was built for.

### 3.5 Logic in Morality and its Upstream Causes

A clarification before proceeding. The claim we are about to make concerns first-order moral
philosophy, i.e. the tradition of arguing for particular moral positions, principles, and codes, 
such as deontology or utilitarianism.
It does not apply to metaethics, whose goal is to analyse what morality is rather than to
propagate a particular moral code. Our own project here is metaethical: we are offering a
descriptive account of the structural features of moral systems, not arguing that anyone ought
to adopt any particular moral preferences. The distinction matters because the functional claim we 
make could otherwise appear self-undermining.

The standard picture in first-order moral philosophy holds that logical consistency is a 
normative requirement on moral systems: the demand for consistency uncovers the deep normative 
structure of morality, and propagation follows as a downstream consequence of having got the 
morality right. On this picture, the relationship runs: morality requires logic. We argue it 
runs the other way. Logic makes for better morality, in a precise sense: logical structure 
makes moral systems more transmissible, more capable of coordinating strangers, and more 
resistant to degradation across generations. The demand for logical coherence is instrumentally 
valuable, not normatively mandatory.

Naturalistic and evolutionary accounts of morality [12, 28, 29] have challenged the standard 
picture, arguing that moral systems are shaped by selection pressures rather than normative 
correspondence. What many accounts in both traditions share, however, is an implicit 
conflation: between what it means for a person to hold a moral commitment and what it means 
for them to hold a logically consistent one, as though rational agency and logical moral 
structure necessarily go together. Our account separates them. Logical consistency is a 
fitness property of moral systems that propagate across persons and generations, not a 
requirement on the moral preferences of any individual. The structural properties Hare took 
to be logically required of moral statements (consistency, universality, overridingness) are 
better understood as selection pressures on moral memes. This explains both why moral systems 
tend to acquire these properties and why the impression that individual moral commitment 
requires logical structure seems so compelling.

The demand for moral consistency is one of the most successful features of the secular moral 
meme -- it is what secular moral systems rely on in the absence of the institutional apparatus 
that religious moral systems have historically used to ensure propagation [14, 15, 16].

This means that moral philosophers demanding logical coherence are participating in the propagation
mechanism rather than standing outside it and evaluating morality from a normative vantage point.
Philosophy faculties, journals, and the tradition of moral argument are doing something functionally
analogous to what the church did for religious morality: maintaining, refining, and transmitting
a consistent moral code across generations. The tools are different -- argument rather than ritual,
publication rather than sermon -- but the function is the same. This is not a criticism of moral
philosophy. Participating in the propagation mechanism of a moral system that enables large-scale
human cooperation and flourishing is a valuable thing to do. A commonplace attitude in philosophy holds 
that a philosopher must follow their premises wherever they lead. We reject this view.

## 4. Objections and Responses

### 4.1 The Frege-Geach Problem

The most immediate technical objection to any non-cognitivist position is the Frege-Geach problem
[17, 18]. If moral statements are not truth-apt, they cannot function as premises in logical
inferences. The argument "killing is wrong; this is killing; therefore this is wrong" requires
that "killing is wrong" carry the same content in the premise as it does when asserted standalone.
If this merely expresses an attitude rather than stating a fact, it is unclear how it can
embed in the conditional at all. Blackburn's quasi-realist programme [4, 19] is largely motivated
by the desire to solve this problem, earning back the right to talk as if moral statements
behave logically, starting from an expressivist base.

We do not attempt to solve the Frege-Geach problem, and we do not think we need to. The inference
does not go through in the way it would for factual statements, and we accept this. "Killing is
wrong but not on Sundays" is a coherent moral preference, and no logical contradiction follows
from holding it, since morality is not beholden to a specific logic. 
Rather than treating this as a deficiency that requires repair, we treat it as reflecting the actual 
nature of moral statements. The intuition that the inference must go through drives both Hare's 
universalisability constraint [3] and Blackburn's quasi-realist project. This is precisely what our 
account explains.

This also means that the Frege-Geach problem, while real as a problem for quasi-realism, is not 
a problem for our position. It is a problem for anyone who wants to combine non-cognitivism with 
the semantic compositionality that logical inference requires. We instead focus on how moral 
discourse acquired the appearance of a logic-bound framework in the first place.

When a person embeds a moral attitude in a conditional argument of the form "if killing is wrong 
and this is killing, then this is wrong," they are not performing semantic compositionality. 
They are executing a rhetorical maneuver: borrowing the psychological authority of deductive 
form to make a moral attitude harder to resist. Our account explains why this pattern became 
so prevalent in moral discourse. Arguments structured as deductions are easier to transmit, 
harder to dismiss without explicit counter-argument, and more resistant to defection than bare 
attitude expressions. The Frege-Geach problem identifies what looks like a logical defect in 
non-cognitivism. On our account, it identifies a structural feature that moral discourse has 
learned to exploit.

### 4.2 The Debunking Worry

A natural objection to any genealogical account of morality is that it is debunking: if we can
explain why people hold moral beliefs by appeal to selection pressures rather than truth-tracking,
we have undermined the standing of those beliefs. Mackie's error theory [13] is the most direct
version of this worry. Mackie argues that the genealogy of moral attitudes shows that moral
statements purport to describe objective moral facts that do not exist, and are therefore
systematically false.

Our position is not vulnerable to this objection in the way that error theory is, because we
make a different starting claim. Mackie thinks moral statements purport to be truth-apt and
fail. We think they are not truth-apt in the first place -- they express preferences, not
claims about moral facts. A genealogical account can debunk a belief by showing it was not
produced by a truth-tracking process. It cannot debunk a preference by the same move, because
preferences were never in the business of tracking truth. Showing that a preference was shaped
by selection pressure leaves its status as a preference entirely intact. The person who prefers
not to kill, and whose preference was shaped by being embedded in a moral culture that propagated
that preference efficiently, has not had their preference undermined by learning this. They simply
have a preference, and they still have it.

The terrain here has been mapped carefully by Street [28] and Joyce [29]. Street argues that 
evolutionary genealogy creates a dilemma specifically for moral realists: if our evaluative 
attitudes evolved under selection pressure, they are either adaptively linked to independent 
moral facts, which is implausible on evolutionary grounds, or they are not, which severs the 
connection between our attitudes and moral truth. Our position is not caught by this dilemma, 
because we deny the presupposition: moral statements do not purport to track mind-independent 
moral facts in the first place. Joyce reaches a related conclusion by a different route, 
arguing that the evolutionary origins of moral attitudes show that moral judgements are 
systematically false, since they attribute irreducibly normative properties that do not exist. 
We take the non-cognitivist alternative that Joyce's analysis makes room for but does not 
occupy: if moral statements express preferences rather than claims about moral facts, they 
cannot be false, and no genealogy can debunk them.

This distinguishes our position from Nietzsche's [25] genealogical project as well. Nietzsche thought
that by tracing the historical origins of moral values, and showing them to be the sediment of power
struggles, resentment, and the will to dominate, we can become free from their grip. On our account, 
genealogy has no such loosening effect. Moral preferences shaped by propagation pressures are still 
genuine preferences, still worth holding and acting on. The history of how they got there does not
change what they are. Moreover, we can choose which ones to adhere to, and which ones to adjust, 
and whether we want to ensure logical consistency among our moral preferences.

### 4.3 The Relativism Objection

A further objection is that our account collapses into relativism -- if moral statements are
preferences without truth values, and different cultures propagate different moral systems, then
there is no basis for saying that any moral system is better than any other. Everything is just
competing memes. We reject this conclusion. That moral statements are preferences rather than truth-apt 
propositions does not mean all moral systems are equally good. Even though moral statements are not 
truth-apt, their effects on human welfare and social coordination are empirically assessable. Some 
moral systems demonstrably enable larger, more stable, and more cooperative societies than others. This 
is a selection criterion that explains why certain moral systems propagate and persist, not a truth 
condition that makes their statements correct. But it is not merely arbitrary either. A moral system that 
consistently produces social collapse will tend not to persist, and we can assess this without needing 
to invoke moral facts. Our constructivist commitment preserves exactly this kind of evaluation: moral 
systems can be assessed by what they produce for human welfare regardless of whether they achieve internal 
logical consistency.

A necessary caveat: our account is descriptive, not normative. It explains which moral systems 
propagate and persist, not which are good. These questions can come apart. A logically coherent, 
universalisable moral system can spread effectively even if its content is harmful to those 
outside the group that holds it. Fitness selects for transmissibility and internal coordination, 
not for the welfare of everyone affected. When we say that some moral systems produce more 
stable and cooperative societies, we mean this about the societies that hold them. Evaluating 
the content of what propagates requires bringing one's own moral preferences to bear.

This is also where the mutually constitutive structure of moral culture becomes relevant. 
The existing attitudes that a new moral meme must fit with in order to propagate were 
themselves shaped by prior moral memes. There is no neutral baseline of raw human preferences 
against which moral systems can be evaluated. The preference landscape is already morally 
shaped: evolution has endowed us with the capacity to care for others and a tendency to 
experience their suffering as our own. Moral change is therefore always revision within an 
inherited tradition, not a leap to a view from nowhere. This is not relativism -- it is a 
recognition that moral progress, where it occurs, is gradual.


## 5. Conclusion

The argument made in this paper is metaethical in character. We have not argued that any 
particular moral code is correct, nor that morality ought to be logical. We have argued, 
rather, that the logical structure typically demanded of moral systems is best understood as 
a memetic fitness advantage: a property that moral systems tend to acquire because systems 
with it outcompete systems without it. The relationship runs not from morality to logic but 
from logic to better morality. Consistency, universality, and overridingness make moral 
systems more transmissible, more capable of sustaining cooperation at scale, and more 
resistant to degradation across generations.

This account integrates several bodies of evidence that are not usually brought together. 
Hare's logical constraints, Dunbar's findings on the scaling of human social groups, Haidt's 
social intuitionist model, and Mercier and Sperber's account of reasoning as a persuasion 
faculty all point in the same direction: the logical structure of morality is the machinery 
of moral transmission, not moral action. Moral philosophers demanding consistency are not 
standing outside morality and discovering its normative requirements. They are participating 
in the propagation mechanism of secular moral culture, performing a function structurally 
analogous to what religious institutions performed for doctrinal morality: maintaining, 
refining, and transmitting a coherent framework across persons and generations.

The account also illuminates a familiar aspiration in legal and political philosophy: that laws
should, ideally, have some moral basis. Law and morality share the same underlying function: both
are coordination technologies for societies larger than personal acquaintance can sustain. Beyond
Dunbar's threshold, law, like morality, makes the behaviour of strangers predictable and enables 
cooperation without shared personal
history, but achieves this via different means: law uses external enforcement, whereas morality 
motivates people internally. Legal systems that ground their authority in widely held moral 
preferences benefit from
the same fitness advantages as morally structured codes: they are cheaper to communicate, more
resistant to defection, and more stable over time than systems whose authority rests on
enforcement alone. Where law diverges sharply from prevailing moral preferences, compensating
enforcement becomes necessary — costly, contested, and fragile. The aspiration that law track
morality is itself a product of selection pressure: legally codified moral preferences propagate
more efficiently than either bare legal rules or free-floating moral attitudes, and the perceived
moral legitimacy of law is among the most powerful amplifiers of that propagation.

Several limitations deserve acknowledgement. The empirical work we have drawn on — Dunbar's 
anthropological data, the moral psychology literature, Sperber's epidemiology of 
representations — is used here as theoretical support for a philosophical argument rather 
than as direct evidence for claims about secular moral propagation specifically. A fuller 
account would ideally be grounded in systematic sociological study: data on how secular 
moral frameworks propagate across populations, how their degree of logical structure 
correlates with their durability and reach, and how institutions such as universities, 
publishing, and formal education mediate propagation in the absence of religious apparatus. 
Such work is methodologically demanding — secular moral preferences are harder to identify 
and track empirically than religious affiliation or doctrinal commitment — but it represents 
the natural empirical development of the theoretical argument made here.

A further qualification concerns the scope of the account. It assumes a degree of 
homogeneity in the community of moral discourse that does not obtain in practice. Different 
linguistic and cultural communities have different colloquial understandings of how logic 
applies to moral reasoning, and different starting axioms within their moral codes. A 
community that grounds moral authority in tradition and precedent applies logical structure 
to moral reasoning differently from one that treats consistency as a primary virtue. Where 
these underlying frameworks diverge — across languages, cultural traditions, or forms of 
moral community — cross-community moral communication encounters a distinctive kind of 
friction. The moral meme must fit existing cognitive and discursive dispositions in order to 
propagate; where those dispositions differ substantially, even a logically coherent moral 
system may fail to cross communal boundaries. The fitness advantage of logical structure is 
not therefore context-independent: it operates within the presuppositions shared by a given 
community, and its effectiveness as a propagation resource diminishes when those 
presuppositions are not held in common.
